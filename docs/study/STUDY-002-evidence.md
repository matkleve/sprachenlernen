## E1 · Retrieval beats re-reading (the testing effect) **[A]**

The best-replicated finding in learning psychology. *Remembering* something
changes memory more than *re-reading* it. Roediger & Karpicke (2006) is the
canonical demonstration; meta-analyses find effect sizes around g ≈ 0.74 for
retrieval practice combined with spacing.

What matters is the direction of the misunderstanding: retrieval *feels worse*
than re-reading, because it is more effortful and produces more errors. Learners
therefore rate the worse method as better. That is why user feedback about
learning methods can only be trusted so far.

> **Product sentence:** every interaction that counts as "learning" must contain
> a retrieval attempt. Looking at a card and turning it over is not practice.

---

## E2 · Spaced repetition beats massed practice **[A]**

The same study time spread across days produces considerably more long-term
retention than the same time in one block. Known since Ebbinghaus, replicated
hundreds of times, stable across ages and materials.

The practically important result is the **ratio finding**: the optimal interval
grows with the distance to the test. Tested in a week, review roughly daily;
tested in a year, roughly monthly. For language learning with no exam date, that
means intervals should grow without an upper bound.

> **Product sentence:** the schedule is the core function, not a setting. See
> [04](STUDY-004-flashcards-srs.md).

---

## E3 · Production beats recognition — but not at the start **[A/B]**

Recognition (multiple choice) overestimates real knowledge relative to gap-fill
tests by roughly 20 %. Recognition knowledge also does *not* transfer reliably
to recall — you can hit a word confidently in a four-option test and still fail
to recognise it in reading when it appears inflected.

The direction of the prompt is not neutral **[B]**:

| Prompt direction | Trains chiefly | Good for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| L1 → L2 ("house" → *casa*) | Form recall — producing the target form | Speaking, writing |
| L2 → L1 (*casa* → "house") | Meaning recall | Reading, listening |

Both are needed, and they are **not the same card**. An app that practises only
one direction builds half a competence and measures it as whole.

The caveat: for a brand-new card, full production recall is too hard. A failed
attempt with no chance of retrieval is wasted effort.

> **Product sentence:** card types are staged — recognition as an entry rung
> only, production as the steady state, both directions on separate schedules.

---

## E4 · Comprehensible input in volume is irreplaceable **[A for the direction, C for the mechanism]**

That massive, largely comprehensible input is necessary for fluency is
consensus. The most popular theory about it (Krashen's "i+1") is, as a
*mechanism*, formulated in a barely testable way and is viewed critically in the
research — you cannot measure "i". What is measurable is **lexical coverage**:

| Coverage of known words | Consequence |
| --- | --- |
| ~95 % | Minimal comprehensibility; reading possible with effort |
| ~98 % | Comfortable, fluent understanding; incidental vocabulary acquisition works |

Converted to vocabulary size (Nation and successors, including Kremmel et al.
2023 replicating Hu & Nation 2000):

| Target | Word families needed |
| --- | --- |
| 98 % coverage, **reading** | ~8,000–9,000 |
| 98 % coverage, **listening** | ~6,000–7,000 |
| 95 % coverage, reading | ~4,000–5,000 |
| 95 % coverage, **listening** (van Zeeland & Schmitt 2012) | ~2,000–3,000 |

Two things follow that shape the product:

1. **Listening is the cheaper entry.** Everyday spoken text needs a fraction of
   the vocabulary reading requires. If you want early wins in comprehension, bet
   on listening rather than reading.
2. **There is a number to work towards.** Vocabulary size is the only competence
   quantity that can be estimated cheaply and reasonably validly — and it bridges
   SRS data and level display. That is the pivot of the entire level model
   ([03](STUDY-003-level-model.md)).

> **Product sentence:** text and audio are selected by measured coverage for
> *this* user, not by a blanket level label. Target: 95–98 % known words. That is
> computable as soon as we know which words they know.

> **⚠ Sharpened 2026-08-08 by [26](STUDY-024-readiness-and-difficulty.md) R5.** The band
> above is a **comprehension** threshold, and the table says so — but using it as a
> difficulty *controller* quietly optimises comfort, because incidental
> acquisition needs unknown words to be present at all. A thousand-word text holds
> about twenty unknown tokens at 98 % and about fifty at 95 %: learning
> opportunities rise as coverage falls, and comprehension falls with them. The band
> stays; what changes is that **a method's purpose picks which end of it applies**,
> written down once per method, and never tuned toward what feels smooth.

## E5 · Explicit explanation works — and beats pattern-bathing alone **[A]**

Norris & Ortega (2000) and subsequent meta-analyses find consistently: explicit
focus on form is more effective than implicit, and the effects are durable.
"Focus on Form" (attention to form within meaning-oriented work) and "Focus on
Forms" (isolated form practice) produce comparably large effects.

Spada & Tomita (2010) add a distinction: explicit instruction helps with
**complex** forms at least as much as with simple ones — the opposite of the
common assumption that the complex stuff has to "come by itself".

> **Product sentence:** short, explicit rule explanations belong in the product —
> but at the point of error and on demand, not as a grammar chapter up front.

---

## E6 · Interleaving usually beats blocked practice **[C — downgraded 2026-08-11]**

Interleaving — mixing task types or categories rather than working through them
in blocks — is the better **practice** schedule in most of the literature, and
blocked practice reliably feels better while retaining worse. That much is E1's
pattern again and it stands.

**What does not stand is the number this entry used to carry.** It read
*"improves above all the ability to discriminate (Pan et al. 2019: d ≈ 0.67)"*,
sourced ◐ — from a citation inside a review, never from the original. A check in
2026-08-11 found that Pan et al. (2019) is almost certainly not a discrimination
result at all but a study of **English speakers conjugating the Spanish
preterite and imperfect** — this app's exact material — whose four experiments
split: blocked numerically ahead in one, no difference in another, interleaving
ahead only in the two that trained across two weekly sessions and tested a week
later. The 0.67 appears to belong to a different paper's *paintings* moderator,
whose **verbal-material moderator runs the other way**.

⚠ **Neither reading is verified here.** The publisher domains are unreachable
from this environment, so the correction rests on abstracts, exactly as the
original claim rested on a secondary citation. The entry is therefore
**downgraded to [C]** and carries no effect size rather than a contested one.
Whoever can open the originals should settle it and re-grade.

**Two moderators this entry never had, and they matter more than the number:**

- **Material.** Verbal material is the class where interleaving does worst in
  meta-analysis; paintings and categories are where it does best. Inflectional
  morphology sits closer to the former.
- **Proficiency.** One line of work reports that low achievers need an initial
  blocked phase to form the association at all; another reports that learners
  with *less* prior knowledge benefit *more* from interleaving. The field has
  not resolved this. Anything here that assumes a direction is inventing one.

> **Product sentence — and it governs one axis only.** A **practice** session
> mixes card types and topics: "possessive pronouns, 40 items in a row" is the
> wrong format for *practising*. This says nothing about the order in which
> material is **introduced**. Sequencing what a learner meets for the first time
> and interleaving what they already have are independent decisions, and every
> study behind this entry fixes the item set and varies only the order within
> it. Reading E6 as a ban on a bounded, labelled introduction is reading it off
> its own axis.

## E7 · Gamification affects motivation, barely competence **[B]**

A meta-analysis (Educational Technology Research & Development, 2023) finds:
gamification raises intrinsic motivation as well as perceived autonomy and
relatedness — but has **minimal impact on competence**. It gets people
practising; it does not make the practice better.

Self-determination theory supplies the distinction that matters in practice:

| Reward type | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| **Informational** — tells you where you stand | Supports motivation without damaging intrinsic drive |
| **Controlling** — "do this or you lose X" | Undermines intrinsic motivation; works mainly on the already unmotivated |

A streak that lapses is by definition controlling. A chart of your own
vocabulary growth is informational.

> **Product sentence:** every display is checked against those two categories.
> Controlling mechanics exist only if the user explicitly switches them on. See
> [08](STUDY-008-motivation.md).

## E8 · Handwriting engages more of the brain than typing **[B — with a caveat]**

Van der Meer & Van Weel (2024, Frontiers in Psychology) measured 36 students
with 256 electrodes: handwriting produces widespread connectivity between
visual, sensory and motor regions; typing barely does. Widespread connectivity
is considered favourable for encoding.

The caveat belongs with it, and a published commentary in the same journal makes
it: what was measured was brain activity, **not** learning outcome. Inferring
"more learned" from "more connectivity" is a step the study does not support.
Behavioural studies of handwriting and retention point the same way but are less
uniform than the popular coverage suggests.

> **Product sentence:** handwriting exercises are a justified option, not
> dogma. That is exactly why they appear in [07](STUDY-007-offline-and-paper.md) as an
> *offer* rather than a required station.

---

## E9 · Dictation and dictogloss work, but not more durably **[B]**

Yu, Boers & Tremblay (2025, *Language Teaching Research*) compared three
listening-based activities for acquiring multiword items. Dictation and
dictogloss beat comprehension questions on the **immediate** test — on the
delayed test the advantage shrank.

That is an honest result with a useful reading: dictation forces attention onto
form, and that attentional boost is real, but it does not replace repetition.
Dictation is an **encoding** technique, not a retention technique.

> **Product sentence:** dictation errors automatically generate cards. The
> dictation supplies attention, the SRS supplies retention — the combination is
> the point, not the dictation alone.

---

## E10 · AI conversation partners work — and correct too little **[B]**

A 2025 meta-analysis (Lyu et al., *International Journal of Applied
Linguistics*) finds a medium effect for chatbots in language learning
(g ≈ 0.608). What helps: opportunity to speak without social anxiety, unlimited
patience, immediate feedback.

The consistently reported weakness: LLM systems **prioritise fluency over
accuracy**. They pass over subtle grammatical errors and can thereby entrench
them. Second finding: learners who are *explicitly taught* how to work with the
AI partner learn more than those who figure it out themselves.

> **Product sentence:** the conversation partner needs an explicit correction
> mode with a visible dial between "let me talk" and "correct everything", and it
> must brief the user the first time. A friendly chatbot with no correction remit
> is a pastime.

---

## E11 · Reading and listening together helps; subtitles are not all alike **[B]**

Reading-while-listening (reading the same text while hearing it) helps because
the audio segments the text into meaningful units — precisely the skill
beginners lack ("the words all run together").

For on-screen text the research distinguishes two cases with different effects:
**captions** (target-language text) help link heard form to written form;
**subtitles** (native-language text) support extracting meaning. The evidence on
which is superior when is mixed — which means offering both, switchable, is the
honest answer.

> **Product sentence:** every audio item has a synchronised target-language
> transcript, and the translation is a *separate*, switchable layer — see
> [05](STUDY-005-input-reading-listening.md).

---

## E12 · The two error sources that affect this study itself **[Meta]**

For honesty, because nobody else says it:

1. **Publication landscape.** Studies of learning apps are predominantly short
   (weeks), measure receptive skills (cheap to test) and often have no pre-test.
   Effects from such designs are systematically optimistic.
2. **The transfer gap.** Almost every effect size above comes from controlled
   tasks. Whether a technique works in daily life almost always depends on
   whether it gets *done* — and the most effective techniques (E1, E2, E6) all
   feel worse than their ineffective alternatives.

The second is the real product brief: **the app has to make the uncomfortable
bearable, not claim the comfortable is effective.**

---

## E13 · Learners believe the wrong thing works — even against their own evidence **[A]**

The finding that governs method selection in [12](STUDY-010-method-cards.md), and the
sharpening of E1 and E6.

Kornell & Bjork had participants study paintings by twelve artists, blocked or
interleaved. Interleaved performed better on the subsequent test. Participants
nonetheless rated blocked practice as more effective — **after** taking the test
on which they had done better with interleaving.

The cause is mechanical: **ease during practice is read as learning.** On top of
that comes a prior belief that one-topic-at-a-time is the right order. A
follow-up paper is titled *On the Difficulty of Mending Metacognitive Illusions*
— showing people their own contradicting data corrects the misjudgement only
partially.

At the same time: freedom of choice over one's own practice raises engagement
and satisfaction (E7), while learners with little prior knowledge or weak
self-regulation are overwhelmed by **too much** choice — that is, precisely
beginners.

> **Product sentence:** user ratings of practice methods must not steer
> selection on their own. Preference and measured effect are kept separately;
> choice yes, but tightly bounded (three options, not fifteen).

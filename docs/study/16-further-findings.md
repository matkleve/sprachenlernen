# 16 · Further findings with product consequences

Four topics that each deserve a paragraph rather than a chapter — but each
changes a concrete decision.

---

## W1 · Learning styles are a myth, and users will ask for them anyway **[A]**

The "meshing hypothesis" — that you learn best when instruction matches your
preferred sensory channel (visual / auditory / kinaesthetic) — is among the most
studied and most thoroughly refuted concepts in education research. Pashler et
al. (2008) reviewed over 70 studies and found essentially no evidence that
matched instruction improves performance. Neuroscientifically, the pervasive
interconnection of the modalities argues against it: input channels are never
separate in the brain.

The genuinely relevant part is the **prevalence**: surveys find 93–96 % of
teachers believe it. Which means users arrive with this expectation, and
convinced.

> **Product sentence [D]:** no "learning style" onboarding, no classification as
> a visual or auditory type. What is legitimate and feels almost the same: ask
> about **goals** ([08](08-motivation.md), M7), about **context**, and about
> **skill emphasis**. That answers the same need for fit without selling a false
> causal story.

Not to be confused with [14](14-accessibility.md): that a dyslexic learner
**needs** a second route is something entirely different from the claim that an
"auditory type" learns better by listening.

## W2 · Language consists of building blocks, not words **[B]**

Formulaic sequences — collocations, idioms, fixed combinations — are
demonstrably **processed faster** than freely assembled language, by native and
second-language speakers alike. They lower cognitive load and thereby contribute
directly to speech fluency: fewer pauses, higher rate, when retrieved whole.
Command of such sequences also raises listeners' *perceived* competence.

One limiting finding: they are probably not stored as rigid blocks but retain
internal structure. So "chunks instead of grammar" is the wrong lesson — it is an
addition, not a replacement.

For us this is the justification for F13 (collocation cards), and it moves its
priority up. Concretely:

- Cards for **word combinations**, not only single words — *make a decision*, not
  *decision*.
- When tapping in a text ([05](05-input-reading-listening.md)), detect that the
  user tapped part of a fixed expression and offer the **whole** expression.
- In the vocabulary estimate ([03](03-level-model.md)) an expression must **not**
  count as n words. Otherwise the figure inflates and the calibration breaks.
  **⚠ This is an open modelling question** and belongs with question 4.

## W3 · Sleep consolidates — less of it is usable than one hopes **[B/C]**

New vocabulary is consolidated during sleep; slow-wave sleep and subsequent REM
sleep play different roles, with REM after slow-wave seeming significant for
integrating new vocabulary into existing networks.

The spectacular variant — **targeted memory reactivation**, replaying learned
words during particular sleep stages — works under laboratory conditions and by
now even at home with an EEG headband (+8.6 % translation accuracy for cued
pseudowords). But: a study with adolescents found **no** effect, and the method
requires an EEG device and real-time sleep-stage detection.

> **Product sentence [D]:** no sleep feature. What remains is banal and still
> useful: an **option** to offer the last review session in the evening, with no
> efficacy claim attached. Anything beyond that would be exactly the sort of
> neuroscientific promise W1 argues against.

## W4 · The desired self motivates, the obligated self creates anxiety **[B]**

The finding that retrospectively supports [08](08-motivation.md) — and that was
missing when that chapter was written.

Dörnyei's model distinguishes three drivers:

| | What it is | Effect on anxiety |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Ideal L2 self** | Who I want to be: the person who speaks this language | **lowers** anxiety |
| **Ought-to L2 self** | What I should be: expectations, duty, avoiding consequences | **raises** anxiety |
| **L2 learning experience** | How the learning actually feels | positive → lowers anxiety |

That is remarkably precise: not "motivation is good", but **the duty variant of
motivation measurably produces anxiety** — and anxiety is among the strongest
inhibitors in language learning, especially in speaking.

A streak that lapses, a league you can be relegated from, a reminder saying "you
have not yet today" — all of these are ought-to mechanics. The
informational/controlling distinction from [02](02-evidence.md), E7 and this one
point independently in the same direction.

**What additionally follows**, beyond [08](08-motivation.md):

- The goal question in M7 should ask about the **desired self**, not a duty:
  *"What would you like to be able to do in this language?"* rather than *"By
  when do you need B2?"* — even though the exam variant must exist, because some
  people have real deadlines.
- The weekly review describes what is now possible, not what is outstanding.
  *"You can now read news texts at this level"* is an ideal-self statement;
  *"you are 400 words short of B1"* is an ought-to statement about the same fact.
- Speaking anxiety is therefore not a fringe topic but a designable quantity. The
  conversation partner without an audience ([06](06-production.md)) is the most
  effective instrument we have for it — and the reason not to defer it.

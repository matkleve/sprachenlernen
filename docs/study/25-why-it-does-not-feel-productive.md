# 25 · Why none of it feels productive

The question this chapter answers, in the user's words: even the apps that take
the research seriously do not *feel* productive — "but maybe I am wrong, are
there studies?"

There are, and they point in two opposite directions, because two different
questions are folded into one sentence:

1. Is the feeling **misleading** — does effective practice feel worse than
   ineffective practice, so that the feeling is a broken instrument? **Yes,
   demonstrably, in randomised conditions.**
2. Is the feeling **correct** — do these products really produce very little?
   **Also yes**, and for reasons that are arithmetic rather than psychological.

Which makes the interesting failure not either answer but the choice between
them. A product that believes only the first becomes condescending: it explains
to the learner that their perception is a cognitive bias, while delivering
fifteen minutes of matching exercises. A product that believes only the second
chases the feeling, and the cheapest way to manufacture a feeling of progress is
a streak. So the useful output here is not "the research says you are wrong" but
a **test that decides which of the two you are looking at** — below, under
[the two-cause test](#the-two-cause-test).

The competitor part of the question is answered by **defect**, not by brand. A
list of brands is stale within a year; the defects are structural and outlive the
products that have them. [15](15-landscape.md) holds the brand-by-brand landscape
and the three corrections it forces on our theses. This chapter does not repeat
it — it asks what exactly is *wrong*, and whether the wrongness is the same thing
as the feeling.

## Part 1 · The critique, by mechanism

### C1 · The optimisation target is stated out loud, and it is not competence **[A for the claim, not for its consequence]**

Thesis 1 has so far been an inference from observed behaviour. It does not need
to be. Duolingo's own Q4 FY2024 shareholder letter reports engagement as the
headline result — DAU/MAU up more than 4 points year over year to 34.7 %, over
10 million users holding streaks of a year or longer — and states the plan for
the following year as continuing to A/B-test, "increasing experiment velocity to
make the experience more social and gamified".

That is a primary source rather than a critic's reading, and it changes the
register of the argument. An organisation that runs thousands of experiments
decided by retention will move toward retention, because that is what the
experiments *can* decide. No individual test is resolved by whether a user can
hold a conversation, because that outcome arrives years later, is expensive to
measure, and cannot be attributed to one change.

Note precisely what this is **not**: evidence that Duolingo teaches badly.
[01](01-duolingo.md) lists strengths that are not negotiable. It is evidence
about what the optimiser optimises — and that is the whole of thesis 1.

### C2 · The unit of practice is an exercise; the unit of the goal is a task

Every app in [15](15-landscape.md) practises pieces: an item, a sentence, a
lesson, a card. Almost none of them ever ask the learner to *do the thing* —
read something they chose because they wanted to read it, say something to
somebody, write a message that has a recipient.

This is the defect the rest of the chapter keeps returning to, because it is the
one that explains the feeling (Part 3) rather than merely being disliked. It is
also the defect this study has been circling from the beginning without naming:
thesis 9 says no app makes anyone fluent, and [21](21-method-catalogue-and-context.md)
puts methods beyond the app in the catalogue. Both are this, seen from a
different side.

### C3 · Progress is denominated in activity, so it can only rise

XP, streaks, lesson counts, crowns — and, in the tools that look most serious
about measurement, known-word counters ([15](15-landscape.md), K2). A number that
cannot fall cannot inform, which is [10](10-antipatterns.md) A1 and A4 restated
from the competitor side. The relevant consequence for *this* chapter: a display
that only rises removes the learner's ability to check their own impression
against anything. The impression is then all they have, and Part 2 shows how bad
an instrument it is on its own.

### C4 · Nobody shows the denominator **[A — both numbers are published]**

This is the single most under-communicated number in the category, and the
arithmetic takes one line.

Cambridge/ALTE's guided-learning-hour bands, from a standing start, are roughly
90–100 hours to A1, 180–200 to A2, **350–400 to B1**, 500–600 to B2. Fifteen
minutes a day, every day, without missing one, is 91 hours a year.

So a perfect year of a fifteen-minute habit buys somewhere around a quarter of
the way to B1 — and that comparison flatters the app, because a guided hour is
instructed time with feedback, which app minutes only partly are. Duolingo's own
commissioned study is the source of the marketing claim that ~34 hours is
equivalent to a university semester; even taken at face value, a semester is not
a level.

**Feeling unproductive at fifteen minutes a day is therefore not a mood. It is a
correct reading of a rate.** And the reason no product shows this denominator is
not oversight: a denominator is the one display that cannot be optimised for
return.

The band is honestly weaker than it looks and the weakness is named here rather
than in a footnote — these figures are English-derived and institutional. Using
them for a German speaker learning Spanish or Italian is an order-of-magnitude
guide, not a measurement, and it is the same class of error as copying frequency
block boundaries between languages ([19](19-milestones-and-map.md)). Hence F190.

### C5 · What is measured is what is cheap to measure

Receptive skills, because a multiple-choice test scales and a speaking sample
does not. Already recorded as [02](02-evidence.md) E12 and applied to our own
figures; it belongs in the list because it is why the efficacy literature reads
more favourably than experience does.

### C6 · The tools that removed the false signal did not add a true one

This is the specific answer to *"even the most science-based ones"*.

Anki is the honest case. Its schedule is real, its algorithm is state of the art,
it flatters nobody. And its entire progress display answers one question: **did
you do your reviews?** The queue reaching zero is a statement about the queue.
Retention percentages are a statement about the cards. Neither is a statement
about you, and neither ever says what you can now do that you could not do in
March.

So the science-based tools are not failing at science. They are failing at the
thing Part 3 shows is load-bearing — and that is not a smaller failure, it is a
different one.

## Part 2 · Where the feeling is wrong, and by how much

### P1 · Performance during practice is not learning **[A]**

Soderstrom & Bjork's review draws the distinction the rest of this part depends
on: performance is what you can do now, learning is the durable change, and
conditions that raise the first routinely lower the second. Any judgement made
*during* practice is therefore made on the wrong quantity.

### P2 · Effort is read as poor learning **[A]**

Kirk-Johnson, Galla & Fraundorf (Cognitive Psychology, 2019) is the most directly
relevant study to the user's question. Across three studies, learners experienced
paired strategies — interleaved against blocked, retrieval practice against
restudy — rated both, then chose one for future use. Mediation analysis: those who
found a strategy more effortful rated it **less effective**, and were in turn
less likely to choose it. In study 3 the more effortful choice produced **better**
long-term retention, the opposite of the ratings. A fourth study ruled out the
act of rating as the cause.

Their name for it is the **misinterpreted-effort hypothesis**, and it is exactly
the mechanism thesis 6 already asserts, now with the causal path spelled out:
effort → *perceived* learning → choice. The middle term is the one a product can
touch.

### P3 · Felt and actual learning can be anticorrelated **[A, outside our domain]**

Deslauriers et al. (PNAS, 2019) is the cleanest demonstration, because it
controls the things that usually explain it away. Students in introductory
physics were randomly assigned within the same course to active instruction or to
polished lectures by well-rated instructors; identical content and handouts;
crossover, so each group had both; 149 students over two runs; the survey
instrument written by an author with no access to the materials. Students in the
active condition **scored higher and reported feeling that they had learned
less**. The authors describe actual and felt learning as strongly anticorrelated,
and attribute it to the disfluency of cognitively demanding work.

The caveat is not small and belongs in the same breath: physics lectures, single
sessions, and a self-reported feeling as the outcome. The direction is what
transfers to a language app; the magnitude should not be assumed.

### P4 · The obvious remedy half-works, which is why it may not be the whole remedy **[B]**

Deslauriers' group also tried the fix. A 20-minute presentation early in the
semester explained active learning, the evidence for it, and specifically that
fluency and the feeling of learning are misleading. By the end of term, over 65 %
of students reported their view of active learning had improved, and 75 % said
the presentation had helped. Encouraging — and weak evidence, because it is
self-report about self-report.

Against it: a 2025 test of exactly this reframing found that framing effort
differently **did** change perceived effort and its relationship to perceived
learning, and **did not** change the decision to interleave. Feeling moved;
behaviour did not.

The rule that follows is a constraint on F185: an explanation of why difficulty
is a good sign is allowed to be **a component** of the treatment and forbidden to
be **the** treatment. A product whose only answer to "this doesn't feel
productive" is a tooltip explaining desirable difficulty has talked instead of
changed.

## Part 3 · Where the feeling is right

### P5 · Self-efficacy is mostly made of the one thing these apps never provide **[A/B]**

Bandura names four sources of self-efficacy: performance accomplishments,
vicarious experience, social persuasion, and physiological state. A model-based
meta-analysis of academic self-efficacy (k = 61, N ≈ 8,965) tested the four as a
set and found **performance accomplishments carry by far the strongest unique
association**, controlling for the other three.

In second-language learning specifically, self-efficacy and achievement correlate
at r = .46 across 40 samples (N = 23,050). A second meta-analysis (74 works)
warns that zero-order correlations inflate this figure, so read the direction and
the ordering, not the digit.

Now put C2 beside that. A drill is not a performance accomplishment. Neither is a
completed lesson, a maintained streak, or a queue at zero — none of them is an
instance of *having done the thing*. A product that never lets the learner
perform the target task therefore withholds the principal input to the one belief
that most reliably predicts persistence.

**So in that case the feeling is not a distortion. It is a correct readout of
something real and absent** — and the desirable-difficulty literature must not be
used to argue it away. Preventing that specific misuse is the reason this chapter
exists.

### P6 · The field's own numbers agree, including about us

- Roughly **60 % of participants dropped out** within Duolingo's own commissioned
  efficacy study — reported in a EUROCALL analysis of that study, which also
  notes the company does not publish course completion rates.
- Independent studies are small and modest. Loewen et al. (2019) followed nine
  learners of Turkish for a semester against a 34-hour target; not all reached
  it, and the lowest was 12 hours.
- Rachels & Rockinson-Szapkiw (2018) found **no significant difference** between
  Duolingo and classroom instruction over 12 weeks — and the abstract concludes
  "this demonstrates that Duolingo® is a useful tool for teaching Spanish to
  elementary students". Worth quoting exactly, because it is E12 caught in the
  act: a null result becoming a marketing sentence.
- A head-to-head of Babbel against Duolingo (N = 59, eight weeks) found **no
  statistically significant difference** in learning gains between the two apps.
- A 2025 longitudinal study of 601 Duolingo beginners over six months treats
  *dropping out* as the outcome variable to be predicted. When a field models
  abandonment rather than attainment, that is a fair summary of where its central
  problem sits.

The fourth point is the humbling one, and it is aimed at this project rather than
at competitors: **between-app differences are not where the variance lives.**
Dose (C4) and whether the learner ever uses the language (C2) dominate anything
we can win by having a better scheduler. That belongs next to K3 in
[15](15-landscape.md) as a second principal risk — being right about mechanism
and irrelevant about magnitude.

## The two-cause test

Two questions about the last fortnight. The first is about effort, the second is
about performance.

1. Did the practice feel effortful, confusing, error-rich?
2. Did you, at least once, use the language for a purpose you would have had
   anyway — read something you wanted to read, said something to somebody, wrote
   a message that had a recipient?

| | **Used it for real** | **Never used it for real** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Felt effortful** | Feeling is an **illusion** (P1–P3). Name it once, keep going → F185 | Feeling is **data** (P5). The missing ingredient is the task, not more effort → F187 |
| **Felt easy** | Probably fine — but check the rate → F184 | **The category's default.** Both signals are wrong at once: no difficulty and no performance |

The bottom-right cell is what most of the market delivers, and it is why the
question "why doesn't this feel productive?" has felt unanswerable: the learner
is receiving two wrong signals simultaneously and has no way to separate them.

One line of this chapter must not be softened:

> **The desirable-difficulty argument may only be used by a product that has
> already given the learner a whole task to perform.** Everywhere else it is an
> excuse wearing evidence.

### The roadmap consequence — **resolved 2026-08-08**

This chapter's diagnosis is that the largest cause of "nothing is happening" is
the absence of whole-task use, and every mechanism this study has for that —
commitments, the method catalogue, the floors — used to sit in **stage 4b**. The
thing most responsible for the feeling was scheduled almost last, on the argument
that a menu needs several methods before it is a menu.

**That argument fell.** Most of the catalogue is methods the app does not host
(thesis 9), which need no engine — so the menu was never blocked on having four
of them. It is now stage 1 ([11](11-roadmap-open-questions.md)), and the
whole-task floor moves with it. The paragraph below is kept because its second
half still holds.

The cheap half does not have to wait. F184's *logging* must start with stage 1,
because practice time cannot be reconstructed after the fact — the same argument
as review history under question 16, and the same failure if it is skipped. And a
single suggested real-use action, untracked and unmeasured, needs no
infrastructure at all. Whether that belongs in stage 1 is question 18 rather than
a decision taken here.

- **Thesis 1 is upgraded from inference to primary source** (C1). It was argued
  from observed design; it can be argued from the shareholder letter.
- **[08](08-motivation.md) has no denominator.** Its "what comes instead" section

## Open

- **Question 17 — is perceived effort a third ledger?** [12](12-method-cards.md)
  keeps preference and effect strictly separate. Perceived effort is neither: P2
  makes it the cause of choice, which is a reason to record it, and merging it
  into either existing ledger would destroy the distinction that chapter exists
  to protect. Undecided, and F189 is blocked on it.
- **Question 18 — does the whole-task floor apply from day one?** At A0 there is
  no real task the learner can perform, so a floor demanding one would either lie
  about difficulty or be dismissed on day two. The honest options are a floor
  that starts once a threshold is crossed, or a real task chosen to be tiny.
  Needs deciding before F187 is specified.
- The dose band is institutional and English-derived (C4). Whether it is
  recalibrated per language, and against what, is question 19.

# 08 · Motivation without self-sabotage

Duolingo proved behavioural design works. It also proved how it goes wrong
([01](01-duolingo.md), D1). This chapter is the attempt to get the one without
the other.

---

## The test: informational or controlling?

From self-determination theory, and the only distinction worth memorising
([02](02-evidence.md), E7):

| | Informational | Controlling |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Says | "here is where you stand" | "do this or you lose something" |
| Acts on | competence experience | avoidance pressure |
| Long run | supports intrinsic motivation | undermines it; works mainly on the already unmotivated |

**Rule [D]:** every display and every notification is checked against this table
before being built. Controlling mechanics exist only if the user **explicitly**
switches them on — never as a default.

The second finding from the same meta-analysis is the expectation-damper:
gamification lifts motivation, autonomy and relatedness, but has **minimal
impact on competence**. It gets people practising; it does not make the practice
better. So: just enough of it to open the door — and no more.

## What we adopt

### M1 · Short units with a visible end **[A]**

The most effective mechanism there is, and the most harmless. Fixed length,
visible from the start, always reachable. See [01](01-duolingo.md), S2.

### M2 · Progress that moves

The level model ([03](03-level-model.md)) supplies a number that changes
measurably day to day (percentage within the sub-level) and an event every few
weeks (a sub-level change). Both informational: they say where you stand.

### M3 · A recurring reminder, but one with content

Not *"You haven't learned today!"* but:

> 12 cards are tipping over today. 6 minutes.

The difference: the first message creates guilt about an omission; the second
describes a state of affairs and its price. **[D]** If a notification cannot be
phrased without the words "you have not", it does not get sent.

### M4 · Streak — as a description, not a possession

The streak is simultaneously the best and the worst mechanism. It works; and it
is by definition controlling, because it lapses.

**Proposal [D]:**

- It counts **weeks with at least three study days**, not days. That survives
  illness, travel and weekends — the three commonest reasons for quitting.
- It is **not** the primary display. It sits below the level, not above it.
- On a break: no mourning graphic, no broken flame, no purchase offer. One
  sentence: *"New run started — your longest was 14 weeks."*
- The previous record stays visible. What you achieved is not taken away from
  you.

## What we do not adopt

| Mechanism | Why not |
| --- | --- |
| **Leagues and leaderboards** | Competition is a main driver of gamification misuse (Mogavi et al. 2022). Produces XP farming with the easiest exercises — the system punishes learning |
| **Hearts / lives** | Punishes errors. Errors are the learning process ([02](02-evidence.md), E1). An app that punishes errors trains avoidance |
| **Purchasable streak freeze** | Making the metric directly buyable devalues it completely |
| **XP** | An activity currency with no relation to competence. What is counted gets optimised — so we count competence |
| **A daily goal in minutes** | Rewards attendance. A goal in *cards* or *reviews due* rewards completion |
| **Loss escalation** ("you lose your place in 2 hrs") | Pure avoidance pressure, and the reason people uninstall learning apps with relief |

## What comes instead

### M5 · The competence moment **[D]**

The strongest motivator in language learning is not a score but the moment you
understand something you could not have understood before. These moments happen
by chance — we can **plan** them:

- A text that was too hard six weeks ago, offered again: *"You gave up on this
  one in March. Try it now."*
- An audio excerpt you understood then only with the transcript, now without.
- The first sentence you understand without looking anything up, in a real news
  text.

That is **experienced competence rather than asserted competence**, and it is
cheap to build because we have the history anyway.

### M6 · The review as a narrative

Weekly, in sentences rather than numbers:

> This week 34 words moved from shaky to solid. Your listening made the biggest
> jump — mostly through the four audiobook chapters. You are still confusing
> *ser* and *estar*; that would be the biggest lever next week.

Four properties: informational, causal (it says *through what*), actionable (it
says what next), and free of any demand.

### M7 · Goals the user sets themselves **[B]**

Self-set goals support autonomy; imposed ones do not. The app asks once: *what
are you learning this for?* (travel, work, family, an exam, reading, interest) —
and that answer then steers card selection ([01](01-duolingo.md), D7), text
topics and the phrasing of the review. A goal that changes nothing anywhere is an
onboarding form.

### M8 · Breaks without punishment **[D]**

An "I'm away for two weeks" switch. No notifications, no streak loss, and on
return a plan rather than a mountain (see [04](04-flashcards-srs.md), the backlog
trap).

That sounds like a feature against our own interest. It is not: the user who can
pause without guilt comes back. The one who opens the app to 871 overdue cards
and a broken flame deletes it.

## The honesty caveat

**[D]** This chapter is the weakest part of the study, and that should be said.
The evidence reliably tells us what harms; it tells us much less reliably what
works *instead*. Duolingo's mechanics are proven and effective; our alternatives
are reasoned and untested.

It is entirely possible that an app without leagues and hearts is simply used
less — and an unused app teaches nobody anything, however correct its didactics.
That is a real risk, not a rhetorical hedge.

So this is among the first things that must be measured
([11](11-roadmap-open-questions.md)) — and measured on return rate **and**
measured progress, because only the ratio of the two answers the question.

  all-German because the study is read and argued with while the code is read
  and executed. Rules out German identifiers; `GLOSSARY.md` therefore carries
  both columns. Open for reversal — question 9 in the roadmap.
- **FSRS over SM-2**, and the deciding reason is *not* the accuracy benchmark.
  It is that stability / difficulty / retrievability are quantities a human can

## Later the same day — method cards

User proposed: a daily choice of practice methods, each shown as a card with its
intensity and what it trains; thumbs up/down after a session; an algorithm
learns from that but can still say "this is important once a week anyway".

Researched it before writing, and the finding changed the design. Kornell &
Bjork: learners rated blocked practice as more effective than interleaved
**after taking a test they had just done better on with interleaving**. A
follow-up paper is literally titled *On the Difficulty of Mending Metacognitive
Illusions*. So the thumbs signal is systematically biased **against** the
methods that work.

### Decisions

- **Preference and effect are two separate ledgers, never combined into one
  number.** Preference (thumbs) governs form — length, timing, share, framing.
  Measured effect governs selection. Chose this over a single "method score"
  because once they are merged the distinction is unrecoverable and nothing
  surfaces the loss. Written into `GLOSSARY.md` as a normative sentence, not
  only into the study.
- **The user's "important once a week anyway" became the Floor**, and it is what
  keeps the whole mechanism from degenerating. Two properties make it a rule
  rather than nagging: it states its reason in terms of the learner's own data,
  and it negotiates over *length*, never over existence. Plus a hard cap of one
  floor prompt per day across all methods.
- **Three options in the daily menu, not a catalogue.** Choice supports autonomy,
  but the same literature says weak self-regulators are overwhelmed by too much
  of it — which is exactly beginners. Rules out a "pick any exercise" screen.
- **Thumbs down asks one follow-up question.** "Too long", "too hard" and
  "boring" have three different fixes and only one of them is about the method.
- **Effect estimation is labelled as statistically weak and deferred (F94/F95).**
  Tiny n, heavy confounding, slow feedback. Population priors first, deliberate
  exploration to get any causal footing at all, uncertainty always displayed.
  Chose saying so over shipping a confident-looking number.

### Decisions

- **HVPT gets its own chapter and becomes thesis 7.** Meta-analysis over 79
  studies: g ≈ 0.92 pre/post, g ≈ 0.67 vs. control, gains generalise to unheard
  words *and unheard speakers*, and perceptual training alone transfers to
  production. Best evidence-to-effort ratio in the whole study: no LLM, no ASR,

  problem — chapter 06's pronunciation section is now explicitly the leftovers.

  every user's history.
- **No sleep feature**, despite a real effect existing. It needs an EEG headband
  and failed to replicate in adolescents. Kept the banal part (evening session
  as an option, no efficacy claim) and said why the rest is out.

## Sixth pass — own content, any language, and the map

User asked for three things and explicitly invited criticism. Two of his three
proposals came back changed.

### Where I pushed back

- **"Text korrigieren" as a listening method: no evidence found.** Searched for
  it specifically. What *is* evidenced and looks almost identical is **partial
  dictation** — one study reports a mean gain of 5.3 against 0.33 for controls,
  and it slightly outperformed dictogloss head to head. The decisive difference
  is that correcting a transcript requires no retrieval attempt while filling a
  gap does. Replaced rather than renamed, and the gaps are chosen (audio-recall
  weak, unsolved contrast, recently learned) rather than random — which turns a
  generic exercise into a diagnosis.
- **"Simplify the text" is the weakest idea in the message.** Simplification
  does improve literal comprehension, and it removes precisely the idiom,
  register and syntax that made an authentic text worth choosing. The literature's
  alternative is elaboration — keep the original, add redundancy. So: a five-rung
  support ladder where rewriting is the last rung and is labelled as a different
  version, not as the text.

### Where he was right and I had been too coarse

- **"How different can languages really be?"** My framing in question 2 implied
  languages are uniformly expensive. Wrong. Almost everything is
  language-independent *code*; what differs is *data*. Stanza covers 70+
  languages for lemmatisation on a single UD-based architecture. Norwegian is
  morphologically **simpler** than Spanish. The real dividing line is not
  European vs. exotic but four specific properties, and only one of them is
  fatal: in agglutinative languages "how many words do you know" stops meaning
  the same thing, and in unsegmented scripts the tokeniser is itself a model.
  Hence the quality tier — at tier C the app works but reports **no level at
  all**, because a count with no defined unit is not a measurement.
- **"Mir fehlt der Überblick"** is the best observation in the message and became
  thesis 8: every product gives a compass, none gives a **map**. The strongest
  consequence is K3 — for any saved item, list the specific words between the
  learner and understanding it. You stop learning "vocabulary" and start
  unlocking a particular episode. It is the coverage calculator read backwards,
  so it costs almost nothing.

### Decisions

- **No podcast catalogue.** The learner brings sources (feed, file, link). A
  catalogue is the expensive version of the same idea and adds nothing
  didactically — and it fits the answer to question 1.
- **Coverage over a sliding window, not the document.** An episode at 78 %
  almost always contains passages at 92–95 %. Suggest the passage.
- **Repeated listening is effective and reported as boring** — a textbook case
  for chapter 12 rather than a playback loop nobody enables.
- **Milestone progress counts stable knowledge, not cards seen.** This is one of
  the few figures where optimising it is genuinely right, which is the exception
  that proves A1's rule.
- **Block boundaries are calibrated per language, not copied.** The literature
  disagrees on where 95 % coverage sits (2–3k vs 4–5k units) and the difference
  is counting unit and corpus, not error. Copying either would show English
  figures to an Italian learner.

## Seventh pass — the adversarial review, and what it cost

User approved the review that `AGENTS.md` requires for Sensitive changes. It
returned **nine confirmed defects, every one reproduced**, while all 29 tests
passed. The process earned its keep in a single run.

### The two that mattered most

- **Answers were being silently discarded.** `step` derived the next state from
  stability alone, so any `again` returned `relearning` — including from
  `learning`, an edge the map does not have. The guard rejected the move and
  `applyReview` threw away the whole review: not appended to the log, `lapses`
  not incremented, `due` unmoved, so the task stayed permanently due and could
  never suspend. That is the most common flow in the product — new card answered,
  forgotten the next day. A brute-force walk found 34 dropped reviews in 3,996
  calls across three classes.
- **Two tests were tautological.** AC-8 compared `project` against `applyReview`
  and AC-9 compared `rebuild` against a hand-rolled fold — but both sides
  delegate to the same function in each case. They would pass against any
  implementation. They were the two tests I had felt best about.

### The rest

Post-update difficulty was computed and then not used, making the ordering my
own algorithm spec calls normative completely inert (worth up to two weeks of
interval on a mature card). Post-lapse stability had no lower bound and
approached zero, which the forgetting curve divides by. Day-rounding broke AC-4
by up to 8× in `relearning`. `project` ignored the guards `applyReview` enforces,
so it could promise an interval answering would not deliver, and showed negative
intervals for suspended tasks. `rebuild` discarded illegal results without
reporting. `suspended → learning` was declared legal but unreachable — no
`unsuspend` existed.

### Decisions taken while fixing

- **Next state is a function of (current state, grade), never of stability.**
  `relearning` means "lapsed from review"; a failing `learning` task stays in
  `learning`.
- **Rounding is conditional on the retention budget**, not on a length
  threshold. Round only while rounding keeps retrievability inside
  `retentionTolerance`. AC-4 now holds by construction rather than by luck.
- **`rebuild` returns `{ task, rejected }`.** A refused review is reported, never
  dropped — otherwise a persisted log and the state derived from it disagree
  about what happened.
- **Zero elapsed time producing no stability growth is a property, not a bug** —
  it is the spacing effect. AC-2 and AC-3 were scoped and AC-17 added, so nobody
  later "fixes" it into rewarding immediate repetition.
- **A suspended task keeps its due date** and is excluded by state. Spec now says
  so explicitly; it previously said the opposite of the code and neither had been
  reconciled.

### Shipped

Nine fixes, AC-12 to AC-17, `suspend`/`unsuspend`, 19 new regression tests
(48 passing, 1 skipped), the criteria split into a child spec to stay under the
line cap, and six new entries in `TRAPS.md`.

### The lesson worth keeping

Before trusting a test, ask which implementation would make it fail. If none
exists, it is decoration. Both of my strongest-feeling tests were exactly that.

## Eighth pass — the form-mastery gap

### Correction from the user

- User, learning Italian: *"nur weil ich [das Wort] verstehe heißt das nicht dass
  ich die Dreiergruppen weiß, weil die teilweise so abweichen und bei manchen
  Wörtern mehr und weniger"* — the three conjugation classes, and plural/singular.

He is right and it is a real hole in the level model. The vocabulary estimate
counts lemmas and **silently assumes the forms come with them**. In Italian
*parlare / credere / dormire* diverge, *-ire* splits again (*dormo* vs
*finisco*), the most frequent verbs are irregular, and nouns have their own
patterns plus invariables like *città*.

The failure it produces is thesis 4 one level down: someone with 2,000 lemmas
who cannot conjugate reads well, cannot speak, and is shown B1 across the board.

### Decisions

- **Form mastery is its own level-1 signal**, never folded into vocabulary size.
  Measured over **paradigm cells** — class × tense/mood × person, plus frequent
  irregulars individually.
- **The form→lemma table must store the cell**: `parliamo → (parlare, 1.pl.
  present)`, not `parliamo → parlare`. Free now because the analyser emits it
  anyway; retrofitting means rebuilding the table *and* re-scoring every history.
  Same class of decision as the counting unit, and caught for the same reason —
  a user asked.
- **The paradigm tables in chapter 07 (Ü3) stop being a side exercise** and
  become the training for a measured quantity.
- Side note worth keeping: the most frequent verbs are the irregular ones, so
  form mastery matters most exactly where a pure lemma count looks most
  confident.

### The other thing he asked

*"Es geht ja nicht nur um einen Vokabeltrainer."* Conceded honestly: everything
built so far **is** vocabulary infrastructure, and vocabulary is the easiest
thing to measure, so it will pull the product toward itself — the same mechanism
this study accuses Duolingo of in chapter 1. The counterweights exist (four
skills, second-lowest rule, coverage routing into real input), but the ordering
is a dependency argument, not a statement of priority, and that distinction
needs restating whenever the vocabulary side grows.

### Shipped

New section in chapter 03, F133–F135, UC-041, glossary terms Paradigm cell and
Form mastery, roadmap note on the table format.

## Ninth pass — speaking without AI, and methods the app does not host

### What the user asked

Two things: what the research says about learning sentences and speaking, and —
the bigger idea — that every method should have an **info page** saying why it
works and what variants exist, including methods that go beyond the app
entirely: *"schreib ein Theaterstück und führe es auf"*, *"verknüpfe dich mit
anderen Leuten die die Sprache sprechen"*.

### The finding that moves the roadmap

**The best-evidenced speaking exercise needs a stopwatch, not an LLM.** The
4/3/2 technique — the same story in four minutes, then three, then two — raises
speech rate, lowers hesitation, reduces errors in repeated parts, and produces
*more* complex grammar in the last round than the first, because after round one
the content is settled and attention frees up for form.

Shadowing likewise: all eight fluency studies in a 2025 systematic review report
positive results (segmental sounds inconclusive — so it complements HVPT rather
than replacing it). Planning time before speaking, same picture.

So speaking sat in stage 5 only because I had coupled it to the LLM partner.
**That was wrong**, and the roadmap now says so: F136–F139 move to stage 4, where
audio and transcripts exist anyway. The conversation partner is the extension,
not the prerequisite.

### The idea that changes what this product is

Method cards said what a method trains, never **why it works**. The info page
fixes that, and it inherits the study's own honesty apparatus: the same [A]–[D]
grade, plus a **mandatory** "what it does not do" section. A page without that
section is an advertisement.

More consequential: the catalogue now contains methods **the app does not host**.
Write and perform a play. Find a tandem partner. Cook from a recipe. The app
proposes, prepares and debriefs — and explicitly does not measure.

### Decisions

- **Unmeasurable methods hold their place through a floor, never through an
  effect estimate.** Without this rule they would be sorted out by the very
  mechanism meant to protect quality — the measurable displacing what counts,
  which is exactly the charge this study lays against Duolingo. This is the
  single most important line of the ninth pass.
- **Self-reported completion is marked as self-reported** and never feeds level-1
  signals. A learner who spends a month on theatre and barely opens the app sees
  no measured growth, and is told that this is a gap in measurement rather than
  in their learning.
- **Drama earned its place on evidence**, not on charm: it reduces foreign
  language anxiety and improved oral fluency where controls did not — with the
  honest caveat that researchers say it is unclear *how much* anxiety is moved.
  Mechanism worth keeping: in a role, the mistake belongs to the character. That
  is the exit from the ought-to self of W4.

### Shipped

Chapter 20, thesis 9, two new sections in chapter 12, F136–F146, UC-042 to
UC-044, eleven sources, and the stage-5 correction in the roadmap.

## Tenth pass — the catalogue itself, and context as the first filter

### The correction

User, after the theatre chapter: *"die Theater-Idee war nur ein Beispiel"*. The
point was not drama. It was that **countless methods exist that apps have not
considered or deliberately avoid because they are hard** — and that the
**learning context** decides far more than I had allowed for: paper and dictation
at home, not on the move; typing at the computer.

Both landed. Two things were wrong in what I had written.

### 1 · Chapter 12 described a mechanism with no catalogue

Twelve chapters about how methods are selected, and no actual list of methods.
Chapter 21 is that list: roughly sixty entries across reading, listening,
speaking, writing, accuracy, vocabulary and the world outside — each with what it
trains, what it needs, and its evidence grade.

The framing that makes it coherent: **apps avoid hard methods for structural
reasons, not out of ignorance.** Slow, error-rich, unmeasurable or off-app all
lower the metrics an engagement-optimised product lives on. A product measured
on competence has no such conflict — so the demanding methods are in the
catalogue *because* they are demanding. That is a competitive position that
follows directly from thesis 1.

Weak methods stay in too, labelled weak (background listening: "kaum Ertrag").
Omitting something learners ask about only makes them ask again.

### 2 · Context was in the wrong place in the menu logic

Chapter 12 had "Setting berücksichtigen" as rule 3 of 4. Wrong, and the reason is
simple enough that I should have seen it:

> **A perfect method you cannot perform right now has an effect of zero.**

So the order is **context → floor → effect → preference**, and context is eight
dimensions (eyes, hands, voice, writing surface, sound, attention, time,
company), not one field with four values. Corrected in place in chapter 12 with
a pointer.

The case that shows why it matters: **the kitchen.** Eyes and hands gone, voice
and time free — the best context of the day for audiobook-with-voice-commands,
shadowing and self-talk, and the worst for everything else this app does. An app
that only knows touch exercises has nothing for the most productive
forty-five minutes many people have.

### Decisions

- **Context is tapped, never sensed.** No location, no motion, no time-of-day
  profiling. One tap is faster than any detection and asks for nothing.
- **The catalogue is data.** Adding a method is an entry, not a release —
  otherwise it stops at ten entries, which is the state of the industry.
- **A long window is a different offer, not more of the short one.** Given two
  hours, every app offers its five-minute unit twenty-four times, which is
  massed repetition and precisely what E2 forbids. UC-048 exists because nobody
  serves this.

### Shipped

Chapter 21 with the catalogue, thesis 10, F147–F155, UC-045 to UC-048 (48 use
cases), three glossary terms, and the menu-ordering correction in chapter 12.

## Eleventh pass — the filter, favourites, and the look

### Filter criteria: eight dimensions are the model, four are the interface

User asked what the best filter criteria would be. Checking the catalogue for
which dimensions actually *separate* methods leaves four: **time, eyes free,
can you speak aloud, what do you write on**. Hands is near-identical to writing
surface; sound is rarely the blocker; attention overlaps intensity and people
self-rate it badly; company matters for a handful of methods and works better as
a switch you turn on than a question you answer.

Even four is too many for daily use, so they are asked **once** and stored as a
named preset. Day to day it is one tap on "Küche".

### Favourites and data

A favourites list is a clean, explicit interface for the **preference** ledger —
more honest than inferring preference from click behaviour. It steers form and
share, never selection alone.

The user's *"wir als Entwickler leben von Daten"* is right, with three caveats
now written into chapter 21: with one user the effect estimate is noise for
months; without an exploration share it is a self-confirming loop; and the
moment other people generate the data it becomes a different regime — purpose
limitation, consent, and whether a method rating needs to leave the device at
all. Decision: effect data stays **local**; aggregating across people is a
separate, explicitly taken decision, not the default.

### Chapter 22 — design, without picking colours

User floated a Claude-like feel with a dynamic serif, and also "maybe very
colourful like Duolingo". Colours are his call and come later, so this chapter
records constraints rather than choices — plus one argument that narrows it:

**The interface makes a promise before anyone reads a word.** Bright, round and
bouncy says *game, easy, rewarded*. This product then delivers a dictation, a
card failed for the fourth time, a level that went down, and a mandatory "what
this does not do" section. Duolingo's look is exactly right for a product
measured on return; borrowing the look without the mechanics is the worst
combination, because the appearance creates the expectation the mechanics then
break.

The inverse fails too — sober and grey produces the app that is didactically
right and never opened, which chapter 08 already names as a real risk.

Target feeling recorded as **"a well-made tool that takes you seriously"**, and
the serif instinct fits for a content reason: this product is text-heavy —
derivations, info pages, explanations at the error point, the weekly review in
sentences.

The two real test cases for any palette, noted for the later session: **how does
a level that went down look**, and **how does "not measured" look**. A palette
that flatters cheerful states is easy; one that renders the honest states with
dignity is the job.

### Chapter 23 — how an exercise runs

The user's second idea, and it comes from modern cooking apps: ingredients as a
checklist, one step per screen, a timer inside the step, swipe freely while it
keeps running — and the detail that matters most: **swiping past a step does not
mark it done.**

That is not a UI nicety. It is the same principle as recording the reveal level
when listening: **passing through something is not evidence of having done it.**
A product that counts screens seen as work performed corrupts its own
measurements — and this one derives a competence level from them.

### Decisions

- **Five step types**: prepare · do · wait · check · decide. The exercise becomes
  data (an ordered list of typed steps) rather than a bespoke screen per method,
  which matters at sixty catalogue entries.
- **`seen` and `done` are separate states, and only `done` feeds the level
  model.** Nothing is auto-ticked by a timer expiring; a timer says how long, not
  whether.
- **The prepare step is the context model made tangible** — paper, headphones, a
  place you can speak. It also fixes the quiet killer of paper exercises: people
  start them, find they have no pen, and stop.
- **The end of an exercise offers, never acts**, and offers exactly two things.
  Declining is a complete outcome.
- **F69 split.** "No photo recognition" was right for dictation, where a key
  exists and self-correction is better. It was wrong for free writing, where
  correction is the whole point and there is nothing to compare against. The two
  cases had been collapsed under one feature number.
- **"Hard is allowed, too hard achieves nothing"** (the user's closing line)
  turned out to name a boundary the study had only implied. Difficulty helps while
  the effort still succeeds — which is already why new cards start with
  recognition and why input targets 95–98 % coverage. Same principle, three faces,
  now stated once.

## Thirteenth pass — speaking as the goal, and what a microphone may claim

Four things from the user, and the research changed three of them. Chapter 24.

### The goal question, and the boundary that answers it

*"The goal should mainly be speaking, but also to get the 20/80 out of it."* Two
goals that look like they compete. They do not — **input is the precondition,
speaking is the goal**, which is one chain and not two priorities, and the
roadmap already runs in that order.

The temptation was to reweight the level model toward speaking. That would have
been the single most damaging change available: a measurement that moves when you
declare a goal is reporting the goal. So the boundary is now explicit and is
thesis 11 — a goal changes the **headline skill**, the **floors** and the
**content selection**, and nothing else. `GLOSSARY.md` carries it as a rule under
*Goal*.

### The landing sentence — the user's idea, made stronger by one correction

*"Maybe a sentence on the landing screen that gets harder with your level."* Good
idea, and buildable: the **CEFR-SP corpus** (Arase et al., EMNLP 2022) is 17,000
sentences annotated by education professionals with an assessment model at
macro-F1 84.5 %, ahead of readability baselines.

The correction came from a second finding. Self-assessment against CEFR can-do
statements is valid overall but biased by proficiency in a known direction —
**lower-proficiency learners overestimate, advanced learners underestimate**. So
the screen must not *ask* "do you understand this?". It asks the learner to **tap
the words they are unsure of**, which is checkable rather than self-reported and
does three jobs at once: a demonstration, an IRT item that quietly builds F24,
and a card capture.

**[D]** The sentence sits one step **above** the estimate. At level it confirms,
above level it informs, and either outcome is worth the second it costs.

### Stagnation — a better marker than "the curve is flat"

The plateau/fossilization literature names four markers, and the useful find is
the second: **alternation between correct and incorrect use of the same
structure**. A flat curve has a dozen innocent explanations — a busy month, a
holiday. Alternation on one paradigm cell has very few, and we already hold that
data per task, per cell.

The most valuable output of the detector turned out to be the one with **no data
at all**: a learner who never speaks does not have a low speaking level, they
have none. Saying that once is the whole feature.

### Went wrong in the design, caught by the user's own example

*"Write to a buddy in the target language from now on"* does not fit the
catalogue. Chapter 21 had assumed every entry is a **session** — duration,
context, completion — and these have none of the three. They are the
highest-leverage, cheapest things a learner can do, and they were structurally
unrepresentable. The catalogue now has two entry types, `method` and
**`commitment`**, and chapter 21 carries the correction at the bottom rather than
being quietly rewritten.

With one rule that matters more than it looks: **no completion tracking and no
streak on a commitment.** A commitment that gets ticked daily has become a method
with extra steps, and the tick is exactly the activity metric A1 forbids.

### The microphone — pushing back on the user's phrasing

*"The phone in listen mode showing 'hearing you well' or badly."* The instinct
underneath it is right and unlocks something real; the display is not.

Automated L2 speech assessment separates **intelligibility**, **comprehensibility**
and **accentedness**. Benchmarked against human leave-one-out agreement — which
is itself only r = .46–.73 — **no large audio language model reached human level
on any dimension**. "Hearing you well" is a comprehensibility rating, the
dimension machines are worst at.

Note what the benchmark implies, though: *humans do not agree with each other
very well either*. So the answer is not a better score. It is to stop scoring:

```
  You read:   "Aunque llovía, decidimos salir."
  I heard:    "Aunque lloviera, decidimos salir."
```

A wrong transcript is visibly wrong; a wrong score is invisibly wrong. And it
generalises into the principle the whole chapter needed: **a hard method becomes
measurable not by grading it, but by capturing a by-product that is objectively
checkable.** Read-aloud works because the text is known, so there is ground
truth. A live input indicator stays — that is a statement about the device, never
about the person.

### Decisions

- **Speaking is the goal** → roadmap question 15, answered.
- **Where the data lives** → roadmap question 16, opened and marked as blocking
  **stage 1**, because that is when review history starts existing in whatever
  shape it is first written in. Recommendation: local first, log shaped for sync
  from day one (append-only, per-review UUIDs, no update-in-place), so a server
  is later an addition and not a migration.
- **The landing sentence lands in stage 2**, with its scoring (F169) deferred —
  showing it does not depend on measuring it.
- **Stagnation detection lands in stage 4b, not stage 2.** Its output is "do this
  instead", and without a menu to point at, the detector can only deliver a
  diagnosis with no treatment.
- **Three of the five sources for this chapter are ◐**, not ⬤ — direction well
  supported, exact figures from secondary reporting. That is why S3 and S6 are
  graded **[B]**.

### Missing from the record, added now

The lexicon commit (`e266057`, stage 0: language profiles, frequency data, form
resolution, `data/`) never got a diary entry at the time. What it decided, for
whoever resumes there: **lemma resolution is a lookup table, not a model** —
morphological analysers are Python and build-time, which is what makes resolution
offline, deterministic and identical on every device, and why adding a language
means generating data rather than shipping code. Both shipped languages are at
**quality tier C** (no lemma table yet), which is derived and not settable, and
tier C means **no level value is claimed at all**.

## Later the same day — method cards

User proposed: a daily choice of practice methods, each shown as a card with its
intensity and what it trains; thumbs up/down after a session; an algorithm
learns from that but can still say "this is important once a week anyway".

Researched it before writing, and the finding changed the design. Kornell &
Bjork: learners rated blocked practice as more effective than interleaved
**after taking a test they had just done better on with interleaving**. A
follow-up paper is literally titled *On the Difficulty of Mending Metacognitive
Illusions*. So the thumbs signal is systematically biased **against** the
methods that work.

### Decisions

- **Preference and effect are two separate ledgers, never combined into one
  number.** Preference (thumbs) governs form — length, timing, share, framing.
  Measured effect governs selection. Chose this over a single "method score"
  because once they are merged the distinction is unrecoverable and nothing
  surfaces the loss. Written into `GLOSSARY.md` as a normative sentence, not
  only into the study.
- **The user's "important once a week anyway" became the Floor**, and it is what
  keeps the whole mechanism from degenerating. Two properties make it a rule
  rather than nagging: it states its reason in terms of the learner's own data,
  and it negotiates over *length*, never over existence. Plus a hard cap of one
  floor prompt per day across all methods.
  > **Corrected in the nineteenth pass, same day.** "Never over existence" was an
  > escalation of the user's remark, not the remark. A floor bounds what the app
  > **offers**, never what the learner owes; "no" is a complete answer. Left here
  > as written, because what was believed at the time is the point of a diary.
- **Three options in the daily menu, not a catalogue.** Choice supports autonomy,
  but the same literature says weak self-regulators are overwhelmed by too much
  of it — which is exactly beginners. Rules out a "pick any exercise" screen.
- **Thumbs down asks one follow-up question.** "Too long", "too hard" and
  "boring" have three different fixes and only one of them is about the method.
- **Effect estimation is labelled as statistically weak and deferred (F94/F95).**
  Tiny n, heavy confounding, slow feedback. Population priors first, deliberate
  exploration to get any causal footing at all, uncertainty always displayed.
  Chose saying so over shipping a confident-looking number.

### Decisions

- **HVPT gets its own chapter and becomes thesis 7.** Meta-analysis over 79
  studies: g ≈ 0.92 pre/post, g ≈ 0.67 vs. control, gains generalise to unheard
  words *and unheard speakers*, and perceptual training alone transfers to
  production. Best evidence-to-effort ratio in the whole study: no LLM, no ASR,
  no scoring of user audio. Rules out treating pronunciation as a production
  problem — chapter 06's pronunciation section is now explicitly the leftovers.
- **The talker pool size is an invariant, not a production parameter.** High

- **Skill profile is configurable, and it lands in stage 2, not later.** A deaf

  is a calculation error, not a display issue, and retrofitting it means bending
  every user's history.
- **No sleep feature**, despite a real effect existing. It needs an EEG headband
  and failed to replicate in adolescents. Kept the banal part (evening session
  as an option, no efficacy claim) and said why the rest is out.

## Sixth pass — own content, any language, and the map

User asked for three things and explicitly invited criticism. Two of his three
proposals came back changed.

### Where I pushed back

- **"Text korrigieren" as a listening method: no evidence found.** Searched for
  it specifically. What *is* evidenced and looks almost identical is **partial
  dictation** — one study reports a mean gain of 5.3 against 0.33 for controls,
  and it slightly outperformed dictogloss head to head. The decisive difference
  is that correcting a transcript requires no retrieval attempt while filling a
  gap does. Replaced rather than renamed, and the gaps are chosen (audio-recall
  weak, unsolved contrast, recently learned) rather than random — which turns a
  generic exercise into a diagnosis.
- **"Simplify the text" is the weakest idea in the message.** Simplification
  does improve literal comprehension, and it removes precisely the idiom,
  register and syntax that made an authentic text worth choosing. The literature's
  alternative is elaboration — keep the original, add redundancy. So: a five-rung
  support ladder where rewriting is the last rung and is labelled as a different
  version, not as the text.

### Where he was right and I had been too coarse

- **"How different can languages really be?"** My framing in question 2 implied
  languages are uniformly expensive. Wrong. Almost everything is
  language-independent *code*; what differs is *data*. Stanza covers 70+
  languages for lemmatisation on a single UD-based architecture. Norwegian is
  morphologically **simpler** than Spanish. The real dividing line is not
  European vs. exotic but four specific properties, and only one of them is
  fatal: in agglutinative languages "how many words do you know" stops meaning
  the same thing, and in unsegmented scripts the tokeniser is itself a model.
  Hence the quality tier — at tier C the app works but reports **no level at
  all**, because a count with no defined unit is not a measurement.
- **"Mir fehlt der Überblick"** is the best observation in the message and became
  thesis 8: every product gives a compass, none gives a **map**. The strongest
  consequence is K3 — for any saved item, list the specific words between the
  learner and understanding it. You stop learning "vocabulary" and start
  unlocking a particular episode. It is the coverage calculator read backwards,
  so it costs almost nothing.

### Decisions

- **No podcast catalogue.** The learner brings sources (feed, file, link). A
  catalogue is the expensive version of the same idea and adds nothing
  didactically — and it fits the answer to question 1.
- **Coverage over a sliding window, not the document.** An episode at 78 %
  almost always contains passages at 92–95 %. Suggest the passage.
- **Repeated listening is effective and reported as boring** — a textbook case
  for chapter 12 rather than a playback loop nobody enables.
- **Milestone progress counts stable knowledge, not cards seen.** This is one of
  the few figures where optimising it is genuinely right, which is the exception
  that proves A1's rule.
- **Block boundaries are calibrated per language, not copied.** The literature
  disagrees on where 95 % coverage sits (2–3k vs 4–5k units) and the difference
  is counting unit and corpus, not error. Copying either would show English
  figures to an Italian learner.

## Seventh pass — the adversarial review, and what it cost

User approved the review that `AGENTS.md` requires for Sensitive changes. It
returned **nine confirmed defects, every one reproduced**, while all 29 tests
passed. The process earned its keep in a single run.

### The two that mattered most

- **Answers were being silently discarded.** `step` derived the next state from
  stability alone, so any `again` returned `relearning` — including from
  `learning`, an edge the map does not have. The guard rejected the move and
  `applyReview` threw away the whole review: not appended to the log, `lapses`
  not incremented, `due` unmoved, so the task stayed permanently due and could
  never suspend. That is the most common flow in the product — new card answered,
  forgotten the next day. A brute-force walk found 34 dropped reviews in 3,996
  calls across three classes.
- **Two tests were tautological.** AC-8 compared `project` against `applyReview`
  and AC-9 compared `rebuild` against a hand-rolled fold — but both sides
  delegate to the same function in each case. They would pass against any
  implementation. They were the two tests I had felt best about.

### The rest

Post-update difficulty was computed and then not used, making the ordering my
own algorithm spec calls normative completely inert (worth up to two weeks of
interval on a mature card). Post-lapse stability had no lower bound and
approached zero, which the forgetting curve divides by. Day-rounding broke AC-4
by up to 8× in `relearning`. `project` ignored the guards `applyReview` enforces,
so it could promise an interval answering would not deliver, and showed negative
intervals for suspended tasks. `rebuild` discarded illegal results without
reporting. `suspended → learning` was declared legal but unreachable — no
`unsuspend` existed.

### Decisions taken while fixing

- **Next state is a function of (current state, grade), never of stability.**
  `relearning` means "lapsed from review"; a failing `learning` task stays in
  `learning`.
- **Rounding is conditional on the retention budget**, not on a length
  threshold. Round only while rounding keeps retrievability inside
  `retentionTolerance`. AC-4 now holds by construction rather than by luck.
- **`rebuild` returns `{ task, rejected }`.** A refused review is reported, never
  dropped — otherwise a persisted log and the state derived from it disagree
  about what happened.
- **Zero elapsed time producing no stability growth is a property, not a bug** —
  it is the spacing effect. AC-2 and AC-3 were scoped and AC-17 added, so nobody
  later "fixes" it into rewarding immediate repetition.
- **A suspended task keeps its due date** and is excluded by state. Spec now says
  so explicitly; it previously said the opposite of the code and neither had been
  reconciled.

### Shipped

Nine fixes, AC-12 to AC-17, `suspend`/`unsuspend`, 19 new regression tests
(48 passing, 1 skipped), the criteria split into a child spec to stay under the
line cap, and six new entries in `TRAPS.md`.

### The lesson worth keeping

Before trusting a test, ask which implementation would make it fail. If none
exists, it is decoration. Both of my strongest-feeling tests were exactly that.

## Eighth pass — the form-mastery gap

### Correction from the user

- User, learning Italian: *"nur weil ich [das Wort] verstehe heißt das nicht dass
  ich die Dreiergruppen weiß, weil die teilweise so abweichen und bei manchen
  Wörtern mehr und weniger"* — the three conjugation classes, and plural/singular.

He is right and it is a real hole in the level model. The vocabulary estimate
counts lemmas and **silently assumes the forms come with them**. In Italian
*parlare / credere / dormire* diverge, *-ire* splits again (*dormo* vs
*finisco*), the most frequent verbs are irregular, and nouns have their own
patterns plus invariables like *città*.

The failure it produces is thesis 4 one level down: someone with 2,000 lemmas
who cannot conjugate reads well, cannot speak, and is shown B1 across the board.

### Decisions

- **Form mastery is its own level-1 signal**, never folded into vocabulary size.
  Measured over **paradigm cells** — class × tense/mood × person, plus frequent
  irregulars individually.
- **The form→lemma table must store the cell**: `parliamo → (parlare, 1.pl.
  present)`, not `parliamo → parlare`. Free now because the analyser emits it
  anyway; retrofitting means rebuilding the table *and* re-scoring every history.
  Same class of decision as the counting unit, and caught for the same reason —
  a user asked.
- **The paradigm tables in chapter 07 (Ü3) stop being a side exercise** and
  become the training for a measured quantity.
- Side note worth keeping: the most frequent verbs are the irregular ones, so
  form mastery matters most exactly where a pure lemma count looks most
  confident.

### The other thing he asked

*"Es geht ja nicht nur um einen Vokabeltrainer."* Conceded honestly: everything
built so far **is** vocabulary infrastructure, and vocabulary is the easiest
thing to measure, so it will pull the product toward itself — the same mechanism
this study accuses Duolingo of in chapter 1. The counterweights exist (four
skills, second-lowest rule, coverage routing into real input), but the ordering
is a dependency argument, not a statement of priority, and that distinction
needs restating whenever the vocabulary side grows.

New section in chapter 03, F133–F135, UC-041, glossary terms Paradigm cell and

## Ninth pass — speaking without AI, and methods the app does not host

### What the user asked

Two things: what the research says about learning sentences and speaking, and —
the bigger idea — that every method should have an **info page** saying why it
works and what variants exist, including methods that go beyond the app
entirely: *"schreib ein Theaterstück und führe es auf"*, *"verknüpfe dich mit
anderen Leuten die die Sprache sprechen"*.

### The finding that moves the roadmap

**The best-evidenced speaking exercise needs a stopwatch, not an LLM.** The
4/3/2 technique — the same story in four minutes, then three, then two — raises
speech rate, lowers hesitation, reduces errors in repeated parts, and produces
*more* complex grammar in the last round than the first, because after round one
the content is settled and attention frees up for form.

Shadowing likewise: all eight fluency studies in a 2025 systematic review report
positive results (segmental sounds inconclusive — so it complements HVPT rather
than replacing it). Planning time before speaking, same picture.

So speaking sat in stage 5 only because I had coupled it to the LLM partner.
**That was wrong**, and the roadmap now says so: F136–F139 move to stage 4, where
audio and transcripts exist anyway. The conversation partner is the extension,
not the prerequisite.

### The idea that changes what this product is

Method cards said what a method trains, never **why it works**. The info page
fixes that, and it inherits the study's own honesty apparatus: the same [A]–[D]
grade, plus a **mandatory** "what it does not do" section. A page without that
section is an advertisement.

More consequential: the catalogue now contains methods **the app does not host**.
Write and perform a play. Find a tandem partner. Cook from a recipe. The app
proposes, prepares and debriefs — and explicitly does not measure.

### Decisions

- **Unmeasurable methods hold their place through a floor, never through an
  effect estimate.** Without this rule they would be sorted out by the very
  mechanism meant to protect quality — the measurable displacing what counts,
  which is exactly the charge this study lays against Duolingo. This is the
  single most important line of the ninth pass.
- **Self-reported completion is marked as self-reported** and never feeds level-1
  signals. A learner who spends a month on theatre and barely opens the app sees
  no measured growth, and is told that this is a gap in measurement rather than
  in their learning.
- **Drama earned its place on evidence**, not on charm: it reduces foreign
  language anxiety and improved oral fluency where controls did not — with the
  honest caveat that researchers say it is unclear *how much* anxiety is moved.
  Mechanism worth keeping: in a role, the mistake belongs to the character. That
  is the exit from the ought-to self of W4.

Chapter 20, thesis 9, two new sections in chapter 12, F136–F146, UC-042 to
UC-044, eleven sources, and the stage-5 correction in the roadmap.

## Tenth pass — the catalogue itself, and context as the first filter

### The correction

User, after the theatre chapter: *"die Theater-Idee war nur ein Beispiel"*. The
point was not drama. It was that **countless methods exist that apps have not
considered or deliberately avoid because they are hard** — and that the
**learning context** decides far more than I had allowed for: paper and dictation
at home, not on the move; typing at the computer.

Both landed. Two things were wrong in what I had written.

### 1 · Chapter 12 described a mechanism with no catalogue

Twelve chapters about how methods are selected, and no actual list of methods.
Chapter 21 is that list: roughly sixty entries across reading, listening,
speaking, writing, accuracy, vocabulary and the world outside — each with what it
trains, what it needs, and its evidence grade.

The framing that makes it coherent: **apps avoid hard methods for structural
reasons, not out of ignorance.** Slow, error-rich, unmeasurable or off-app all
lower the metrics an engagement-optimised product lives on. A product measured
on competence has no such conflict — so the demanding methods are in the
catalogue *because* they are demanding. That is a competitive position that
follows directly from thesis 1.

Weak methods stay in too, labelled weak (background listening: "kaum Ertrag").
Omitting something learners ask about only makes them ask again.

### 2 · Context was in the wrong place in the menu logic

Chapter 12 had "Setting berücksichtigen" as rule 3 of 4. Wrong, and the reason is
simple enough that I should have seen it:

> **A perfect method you cannot perform right now has an effect of zero.**

So the order is **context → floor → effect → preference**, and context is eight
dimensions (eyes, hands, voice, writing surface, sound, attention, time,
company), not one field with four values. Corrected in place in chapter 12 with
a pointer.

The case that shows why it matters: **the kitchen.** Eyes and hands gone, voice
and time free — the best context of the day for audiobook-with-voice-commands,
shadowing and self-talk, and the worst for everything else this app does. An app
that only knows touch exercises has nothing for the most productive
forty-five minutes many people have.

### Decisions

- **Context is tapped, never sensed.** No location, no motion, no time-of-day
  profiling. One tap is faster than any detection and asks for nothing.
- **The catalogue is data.** Adding a method is an entry, not a release —
  otherwise it stops at ten entries, which is the state of the industry.
- **A long window is a different offer, not more of the short one.** Given two
  hours, every app offers its five-minute unit twenty-four times, which is
  massed repetition and precisely what E2 forbids. UC-048 exists because nobody
  serves this.

### Shipped

Chapter 21 with the catalogue, thesis 10, F147–F155, UC-045 to UC-048 (48 use
cases), three glossary terms, and the menu-ordering correction in chapter 12.

## Eleventh pass — the filter, favourites, and the look

### Filter criteria: eight dimensions are the model, four are the interface

User asked what the best filter criteria would be. Checking the catalogue for
which dimensions actually *separate* methods leaves four: **time, eyes free,
can you speak aloud, what do you write on**. Hands is near-identical to writing
surface; sound is rarely the blocker; attention overlaps intensity and people
self-rate it badly; company matters for a handful of methods and works better as
a switch you turn on than a question you answer.

Even four is too many for daily use, so they are asked **once** and stored as a
named preset. Day to day it is one tap on "Küche".

### Favourites and data

A favourites list is a clean, explicit interface for the **preference** ledger —
more honest than inferring preference from click behaviour. It steers form and
share, never selection alone.

The user's *"wir als Entwickler leben von Daten"* is right, with three caveats
now written into chapter 21: with one user the effect estimate is noise for
months; without an exploration share it is a self-confirming loop; and the
moment other people generate the data it becomes a different regime — purpose
limitation, consent, and whether a method rating needs to leave the device at
all. Decision: effect data stays **local**; aggregating across people is a
separate, explicitly taken decision, not the default.

### Chapter 22 — design, without picking colours

User floated a Claude-like feel with a dynamic serif, and also "maybe very
colourful like Duolingo". Colours are his call and come later, so this chapter
records constraints rather than choices — plus one argument that narrows it:

**The interface makes a promise before anyone reads a word.** Bright, round and
bouncy says *game, easy, rewarded*. This product then delivers a dictation, a
card failed for the fourth time, a level that went down, and a mandatory "what
this does not do" section. Duolingo's look is exactly right for a product
measured on return; borrowing the look without the mechanics is the worst
combination, because the appearance creates the expectation the mechanics then
break.

The inverse fails too — sober and grey produces the app that is didactically
right and never opened, which chapter 08 already names as a real risk.

Target feeling recorded as **"a well-made tool that takes you seriously"**, and
the serif instinct fits for a content reason: this product is text-heavy —
derivations, info pages, explanations at the error point, the weekly review in
sentences.

The two real test cases for any palette, noted for the later session: **how does
a level that went down look**, and **how does "not measured" look**. A palette
that flatters cheerful states is easy; one that renders the honest states with
dignity is the job.

### Chapter 23 — how an exercise runs

The user's second idea, and it comes from modern cooking apps: ingredients as a
checklist, one step per screen, a timer inside the step, swipe freely while it
keeps running — and the detail that matters most: **swiping past a step does not
mark it done.**

That is not a UI nicety. It is the same principle as recording the reveal level
when listening: **passing through something is not evidence of having done it.**
A product that counts screens seen as work performed corrupts its own
measurements — and this one derives a competence level from them.

### Decisions

- **Five step types**: prepare · do · wait · check · decide. The exercise becomes
  data (an ordered list of typed steps) rather than a bespoke screen per method,
  which matters at sixty catalogue entries.
- **`seen` and `done` are separate states, and only `done` feeds the level
  model.** Nothing is auto-ticked by a timer expiring; a timer says how long, not
  whether.
- **The prepare step is the context model made tangible** — paper, headphones, a
  place you can speak. It also fixes the quiet killer of paper exercises: people
  start them, find they have no pen, and stop.
- **The end of an exercise offers, never acts**, and offers exactly two things.
  Declining is a complete outcome.
- **F69 split.** "No photo recognition" was right for dictation, where a key
  exists and self-correction is better. It was wrong for free writing, where
  correction is the whole point and there is nothing to compare against. The two
  cases had been collapsed under one feature number.
- **"Hard is allowed, too hard achieves nothing"** (the user's closing line)
  turned out to name a boundary the study had only implied. Difficulty helps while
  the effort still succeeds — which is already why new cards start with
  recognition and why input targets 95–98 % coverage. Same principle, three faces,
  now stated once.

## Thirteenth pass — speaking as the goal, and what a microphone may claim

Four things from the user, and the research changed three of them. Chapter 24.

### The goal question, and the boundary that answers it

*"The goal should mainly be speaking, but also to get the 20/80 out of it."* Two
goals that look like they compete. They do not — **input is the precondition,
speaking is the goal**, which is one chain and not two priorities, and the
roadmap already runs in that order.

The temptation was to reweight the level model toward speaking. That would have
been the single most damaging change available: a measurement that moves when you
declare a goal is reporting the goal. So the boundary is now explicit and is
thesis 11 — a goal changes the **headline skill**, the **floors** and the
**content selection**, and nothing else. `GLOSSARY.md` carries it as a rule under
*Goal*.

### The landing sentence — the user's idea, made stronger by one correction

*"Maybe a sentence on the landing screen that gets harder with your level."* Good
idea, and buildable: the **CEFR-SP corpus** (Arase et al., EMNLP 2022) is 17,000
sentences annotated by education professionals with an assessment model at
macro-F1 84.5 %, ahead of readability baselines.

The correction came from a second finding. Self-assessment against CEFR can-do
statements is valid overall but biased by proficiency in a known direction —
**lower-proficiency learners overestimate, advanced learners underestimate**. So
the screen must not *ask* "do you understand this?". It asks the learner to **tap
the words they are unsure of**, which is checkable rather than self-reported and
does three jobs at once: a demonstration, an IRT item that quietly builds F24,
and a card capture.

**[D]** The sentence sits one step **above** the estimate. At level it confirms,
above level it informs, and either outcome is worth the second it costs.

### Stagnation — a better marker than "the curve is flat"

The plateau/fossilization literature names four markers, and the useful find is
the second: **alternation between correct and incorrect use of the same
structure**. A flat curve has a dozen innocent explanations — a busy month, a
holiday. Alternation on one paradigm cell has very few, and we already hold that
data per task, per cell.

The most valuable output of the detector turned out to be the one with **no data
at all**: a learner who never speaks does not have a low speaking level, they
have none. Saying that once is the whole feature.

### Went wrong in the design, caught by the user's own example

*"Write to a buddy in the target language from now on"* does not fit the
catalogue. Chapter 21 had assumed every entry is a **session** — duration,
context, completion — and these have none of the three. They are the
highest-leverage, cheapest things a learner can do, and they were structurally
unrepresentable. The catalogue now has two entry types, `method` and
**`commitment`**, and chapter 21 carries the correction at the bottom rather than
being quietly rewritten.

With one rule that matters more than it looks: **no completion tracking and no
streak on a commitment.** A commitment that gets ticked daily has become a method
with extra steps, and the tick is exactly the activity metric A1 forbids.

### The microphone — pushing back on the user's phrasing

*"The phone in listen mode showing 'hearing you well' or badly."* The instinct
underneath it is right and unlocks something real; the display is not.

Automated L2 speech assessment separates **intelligibility**, **comprehensibility**
and **accentedness**. Benchmarked against human leave-one-out agreement — which
is itself only r = .46–.73 — **no large audio language model reached human level
on any dimension**. "Hearing you well" is a comprehensibility rating, the
dimension machines are worst at.

Note what the benchmark implies, though: *humans do not agree with each other
very well either*. So the answer is not a better score. It is to stop scoring:

```
  You read:   "Aunque llovía, decidimos salir."
  I heard:    "Aunque lloviera, decidimos salir."
```

A wrong transcript is visibly wrong; a wrong score is invisibly wrong. And it
generalises into the principle the whole chapter needed: **a hard method becomes
measurable not by grading it, but by capturing a by-product that is objectively
checkable.** Read-aloud works because the text is known, so there is ground
truth. A live input indicator stays — that is a statement about the device, never
about the person.

### Decisions

- **Speaking is the goal** → roadmap question 15, answered.
- **Where the data lives** → roadmap question 16, opened and marked as blocking
  **stage 1**, because that is when review history starts existing in whatever
  shape it is first written in. Recommendation: local first, log shaped for sync
  from day one (append-only, per-review UUIDs, no update-in-place), so a server
  is later an addition and not a migration.
- **The landing sentence lands in stage 2**, with its scoring (F169) deferred —
  showing it does not depend on measuring it.
- **Stagnation detection lands in stage 4b, not stage 2.** Its output is "do this
  instead", and without a menu to point at, the detector can only deliver a
  diagnosis with no treatment.
- **Three of the five sources for this chapter are ◐**, not ⬤ — direction well
  supported, exact figures from secondary reporting. That is why S3 and S6 are
  graded **[B]**.

### Missing from the record, added now

The lexicon commit (`e266057`, stage 0: language profiles, frequency data, form
resolution, `data/`) never got a diary entry at the time. What it decided, for
whoever resumes there: **lemma resolution is a lookup table, not a model** —
morphological analysers are Python and build-time, which is what makes resolution
offline, deterministic and identical on every device, and why adding a language
means generating data rather than shipping code. Both shipped languages are at
**quality tier C** (no lemma table yet), which is derived and not settable, and
tier C means **no level value is claimed at all**.

## Fourteenth pass — the form→lemma tables, Spanish and Italian to tier B

The build promised at the end of the last pass: real paradigm data for both
languages, with paradigm cells stored alongside every mapping — the direct
answer to the user's own objection that a lemma is not its forms.

### Sourcing, and a wrong assumption caught before it shipped

The plan was UniMorph for both languages. Spanish is complete there — 750 verb
lemmas, full paradigms. **Italian is not**: UniMorph's Italian sample has no
entry for `essere`, `avere` or `parlare`, which is disqualifying rather than a
gap to patch around. Switched to **Morph-it!** (Baroni & Zanchetta, Università di
Bologna) — a full-form lexicon that covers verbs, nouns, adjectives *and*
function words, including the irregulars UniMorph was missing.

Both are complemented by **Universal Dependencies treebanks** (2–4 per
language), which supply what a paradigm list has no entries for at all —
function words — and, from multiword-token annotations, the decomposition of
fused forms: Spanish `del`/`al`, Italian `alla`/`della`/`del`.

### Two bugs a naive pipeline would have shipped silently

**Lemmatiser hallucination.** An early version trusted any lemma the treebank's
own lemmatiser proposed. Result: Spanish gained verbs `comir`, `confunder`,
`estener` — none of which is a real word, all back-formed by a lemmatiser
guessing at an unfamiliar inflected form. Fixed with a guard: an open-class
lemma (verb/noun/adjective) from the treebank must be independently attested —
known to the paradigm source, or seen as a surface form elsewhere in the corpus.
Real verbs the paradigm source happens to lack (`tener`, missing from UniMorph)
still get in, because the guard checks attestation, not source membership.

**A systematically wrong tie-break.** Italian's 1st/2nd-plural indicative and
imperative are genuinely homophonous — `parliamo` is both "we speak" and "let's
speak". Where a treebank has zero attestations of either reading, the two tie,
and the alphabetical fallback picked `imp` over `ind` (`i-m` < `i-n`) — backwards,
since the indicative is the overwhelmingly likelier reading out of context.
Caught by a test asserting the *same* cell name across three conjugation
classes (`parliamo`/`dormiamo`/`temiamo`, all `ind.pres.1pl`) — `temiamo` came
back `imp.1pl` while the other two didn't, because their treebank counts
happened to be unequal and `temere`'s were both zero. Fixed with an explicit
mood-markedness tie-break (ind < sub < cond < imp) ahead of the alphabetical
fallback, which alphabetical order can never be trusted to approximate.

### What the library side needed, and the discipline that found the ambiguity gap

Red phase done for real: extended `lib/lexicon.test.ts` first (36 new cases),
then dropped in a **naive stub** `resolve()` that only ever returned the first
analysis and a `loadLemmaTable` that accepted anything — 16 of 69 failed, not
because the stub was broken but because it could not express what the real
answer needed to say. Three shapes turned out to be genuinely different and
collapsing them was the bug the old single-lemma-table shape was already
carrying:

- **`single`** — exactly one analysis, safe to trust outright.
- **`ambiguous`** — more than one; every analysis is returned, likeliest first,
  so a caller taking the first takes a stated guess rather than a silent one.
- **`fused`** — not a competing reading at all. `del` is *de* and *el* both
  present, and reporting it as `ambiguous` would invite a caller to "pick one"
  of two things that are true together. Checked first, wins outright.

`loadLemmaTable` rejects rather than repairs: a table with one malformed entry
(wrong-shaped analysis, unknown part of speech, a raw source tag as a cell,
wrong `language`) is refused wholesale, naming the offending entry — the fix
belongs in the generator, never patched around at load time.

### Went wrong in the harness, not the design

Two file-length gates (`max-lines: 300`) failed after the real implementation
landed — `scripts/build-lemma-tables.mjs` at 531 lines,
then `lib/lexicon.ts` at 304 after the first fix freed up headroom elsewhere.
Both split along their actual seams rather than arbitrarily: the generator into
`scripts/lemma/{languages,cells,unimorph,morphit,treebank,io,assemble}.mjs`
(one file per source format, one for the shared cell vocabulary, one for
orchestration); the library into `lib/lexicon.ts` (text/frequency/tokenising)
and the new `lib/lemma-table.ts` (the generated table's shape and validation) —
a different concern from the first half even though both sit under the same
spec. Regenerated both tables afterward and diffed them against the pre-split
output: byte-identical, confirming the split changed no behaviour.

### Decisions

- **Both languages are now quality tier B**, not C — `qualityTier` derives this
  from the profile's `lemmaTable` field, now non-null for both. A level value is
  reportable for the first time, with a widened uncertainty band; tier A still
  needs a dated calibration.
- **Incompleteness is measured and shipped with the table**, not assumed away:
  `seedCoverage` and `verbParadigmsComplete` (Spanish 95.6% / 98%, Italian
  93.7% / 100%, against the top 5,000 frequency forms). What a partial paradigm
  means for form mastery is a new **⚠ SPEC GAP** rather than a guessed rule.
- **Missing forms are never generated from regular patterns**, even obvious
  ones. A generated form is a model output inside a table that claims to be a
  lookup, and it would be wrong precisely for the irregular verbs a learner most
  needs — stated explicitly in the spec's Open section so nobody "improves"
  coverage that way later.
- **AC-11 to AC-22 added** to a new split-child
  `docs/specs/service/lexicon.acceptance-criteria.md`, and the parent spec's
  quality-tier table updated to match — the shipped-profile AC now asserts
  tier B, not C.

## Fifteenth pass — why nothing feels productive, and an implementation queue

Two sessions ran in parallel today. This one answered a question the study had no
home for, then audited what actually exists in `app/`.

### The question

The user: even the apps that take the research seriously do not *feel*
productive — "but maybe I am wrong, are there studies?" There are, and they point
both ways, which is the finding rather than an inconvenience. Chapter 25.

- **The feeling is a bad instrument.** Deslauriers et al. (PNAS 2019): randomised
  crossover, identical content, 149 students — the active condition scored higher
  and reported feeling it had learned *less*, strongly anticorrelated.
  Kirk-Johnson et al. (2019) supply the mechanism: effort → perceived learning →
  choice, with the more effortful strategy retaining better than its ratings.
- **And the feeling is correct.** Cambridge puts B1 at 350–400 guided hours;
  fifteen minutes a day is 91 hours a year. Feeling unproductive at that rate is
  a correct reading of a rate. Separately, a model-based meta-analysis of
  self-efficacy finds **performance accomplishments** dominate the four sources —
  and a drill is not a performance accomplishment. An app that never lets you use
  the language withholds the main input of the belief that predicts persistence.

### Decisions

- **The two-cause test, not a verdict.** Effort plus real use → the feeling is an
  illusion, name it once. Effort with no real use → the feeling is data, and what
  is missing is a task. The most valuable line is the prohibition:
  **the desirable-difficulty argument may only be used by a product that has
  already given the learner a whole task.** Otherwise it is an excuse wearing
  evidence, and this study has enough literature at hand to dress it convincingly.
- **Thesis 1 upgraded from inference to primary source.** Duolingo's own Q4 FY24
  shareholder letter reports DAU/MAU and year-long streaks as the result and
  states more gamification as the plan. No longer a reading of their design.
- **A second principal risk, aimed at us.** A head-to-head of Babbel against
  Duolingo found no significant difference in gains. Between-app differences are
  not where the outcome variance lives, which makes "our scheduler is better" a
  weaker position than chapter 15 implied.
- **The critique is organised by defect, not by brand**, so it outlives the
  products, and so it has exactly one owner — chapter 15 keeps the landscape.

### Went wrong — found by audit, not by a gate

- **Tier B meant two different things.** Chapter 18 defined it as "anchors
  estimated, content generated and unchecked"; the spec and the shipped code
  define it as "a form→lemma table exists". The fourteenth pass reached tier B
  with no generated content in existence, which is what exposed the conflation.
  Fixed in chapter 18 and F124, spec side left alone — the spec was right, and it
  is the version a program can compute rather than assert.
- **Two service specs were still `draft`** while carrying 48 and 69 passing tests
  and, in the scheduler's case, a completed adversarial review. Now `active`.
- **The diary grew a second `## Open` section** and the first was left empty when
  the fourteenth pass was appended into it. Removed. Worth noting only because it
  is the two-parallel-sessions failure in its mildest possible form.
- **Dialog never wired `aria-describedby`.** The description renders and is not
  announced, on the component whose main job is confirming destructive actions.
  Its title id is also the hardcoded string `dialog-title`, so two dialogs
  collide. Both invisible to `check:tokens`, `check:contrast` and axe-as-run.
  Filed as T-01 rather than fixed here, to keep this pass docs-only.

### The audit's actual finding

`app/page.tsx` still says *"Grundriss — The plan comes before the building"*.
There are two well-tested libraries and **no product**: no screen a learner could
open, so no interaction flow to audit at all. That reframes the request — the
audit became about what the first flow must be, plus the interaction debt already
sitting in the primitives (Input and Select ship three of the five required
states, and `select.md` documents three, so the spec contradicts boundary 4 in
writing rather than by accident).

Recorded in [`../IMPLEMENTATION-PLAN.md`](../IMPLEMENTATION-PLAN.md), which owns
the code queue while the roadmap keeps owning the product order. It splits the
work by *who can safely do it*: Track A is mechanical and needs no decisions,
Track B contains the tasks where a low-inference agent would invent a rule. The
one structural insight worth keeping: with low-reasoning implementers, the
decisions have to move out of the implementation and into the spec, so
spec-writing becomes the bottleneck rather than coding capacity — and right now
there is about one afternoon of Track A work in the whole repository.

Also learned, and it belongs in any brief: **the gate makes spec-first mean
spec-and-test-together**, because `check:specs` requires a spec's `## Check` to
name a test file that exists. An agent told only "write the spec first" produces
a red gate and starts guessing.

## Sixteenth pass — where the data lives, answered

## Seventeenth pass — the account is required, and what that cost

I had asked one question about ADR-0005 rather than assuming: does signing in ever
become mandatory. The answer came back inside the hour — **yes, an account is
required** — which is why the question was worth asking at all. It reverses a
clause that was fifteen minutes old.

[ADR-0006](../adr/0006-require-an-account.md) records it, and 0005 keeps its
reasoning with one line of status changed. The repo's own ADR rule earned its keep
here: *write the new record, mark the old superseded, never edit history to look
consistent.* Rewriting 0005 to pretend it had always said "required" would have
destroyed the one genuinely useful artefact in it — the argument the evidence
favours, which is the argument to re-read if the requirement is ever relaxed.

**What actually changed, and it is more than a flag.** The row owner is non-null
from the first row, which kills the nullable-owner property. The server therefore
lives inside stage 1, which now carries signup, sign-in, session handling and the
access-control test `BACKEND.md` §8 calls the highest-value test in the product.
Track B inverted: authentication went from last to first, because nothing that
persists can precede the owner it writes against. And the provider question,
which 0005 deliberately deferred on the grounds that sync was distant, is now the
single thing blocking Track B.

**The part I refused to smooth over.** This contradicts `STUDY-001-duolingo.md` S1,
graded **[A]**: every step between "app opened" and "first exercise" costs users
permanently, and the chapter names account creation among them. UC-011 carried "no
account is required to start" as an acceptance criterion. That criterion is now
gone, and the honest reconciliation is question 1 — a tool for its author first,
so the friction is paid once by someone already committed. The cost is deferred,
not removed, so the ADR names the trigger to reopen it rather than leaving it to
be rediscovered: **the first time the product tries to gain a user who is not its
author.**

UC-011 came out *stricter* rather than looser, which was the useful surprise.
If the account consumes the entire budget S1 allows, then nothing else may sit
beside it: signup and the language pair are the only two things asked before the
first exercise, and signup asks for nothing beyond what authentication needs — no
name, no goal, no survey. A decision that removes a criterion has to add the one
that replaces it, or the constraint simply evaporates.

Also worth noting for next time: `BACKEND.md` had been rewritten an hour earlier
to say the choice was half-made and §2 did not apply yet. Both sentences were
wrong within the hour. A doc that states what has been decided is more useful than
one that hedges, *and* it is the doc most likely to go stale — so it belongs in the
same sweep as the ADR, every time, not in a later cleanup.

## Nineteenth pass — two corrections that moved the product

Both from the user, both invalidating something a previous pass had written with
confidence.

### 1 · The floor was never meant to be an obligation

*"I never talked about the concept of say you need to do this daily or this like
that — it should nudge you according to this, but you don't have to do it."*

Chapter 12 had built the floor into a mechanism that "negotiates over length,
never over existence", with the flourish *"a floor you can click away is not
one"*. And it had attributed the whole thing to the user, via their *"this is
important once a week anyway"*. That attribution is what makes this worth
recording: the remark was real, and the mechanism built on top of it went well
past what the remark said. A quote in a doc is not a mandate for everything
built in its name.

**The fix turned out to be a definition, not a redesign.** The floor does two
jobs, and only one of them was wrong:

- **Keeping a method *offered* regardless of preference or measurability** (F145,
  thesis 9). Untouched. This is the job that stops the catalogue converging on the
  pleasant, and offering something obliges nobody.
- **Prompting when one is overdue.** This is where the obligation had crept in.
  Now: it justifies itself from the learner's own data, offers a shorter variant
  because "too long" is the commonest real reason — and then **"no" is a complete
  answer**, with no second ask and nothing resembling a broken streak.

So "floor" survives as a term with a tightened meaning: **a floor on what the app
offers, never on what the learner owes.** No rename needed, which is the cheapest
possible outcome for a concept referenced across fifteen files.

The honest consequence, and the part that keeps the architecture intact:
persistently declining a method means the skill it feeds reads **"not measured"**
rather than being guessed. That is already UC-010's rule for a method the learner
deliberately hides, so non-compliance simply inherits it. The app loses a claim;
the learner loses nothing. Notably E7 was on the user's side all along — chapter
12 had cited it while arguing for the unclickable version, which is the opposite
of what self-determination theory says.

### 2 · Flashcards is one method among many, and the menu is the product

*"Flashcards is also just a method, it's nothing special, it's on the same level
— maybe generally more important. The app is just about: okay, here's your
progress, how much time do you have now, what can you do, those methods would
work this well or badly, here is the evidence, here are different options,
start."*

The roadmap said otherwise in structure rather than in words: stage 1 was
"Flashcards with a glass-walled schedule" and method choice was **stage 4b**, on
the argument that *"a menu needs at least four methods to choose from — before
that the menu is a list with one entry."*

**That argument was wrong, and its own catalogue disproves it.** Most of chapter
21's ~60 entries are methods the app does not host — thesis 9, proposed and
prepared and debriefed but never measured. They need no engine. A menu shipping
with one app-hosted engine still offers a full catalogue of real options. The menu
was never blocked on four engines; it was blocked on an assumption nobody checked
against the chapter two files away.

What moved: the menu, method cards, info pages with evidence grades, the context
filter, the off-app methods and the floors are now **stage 1**, and roughly twenty
features went V2 → V1. What stayed in 4b is the half that genuinely needs
accumulated data — the effect estimate (F94), exploration (F95) and stagnation
detection. Until F94 exists the menu ranks by context, floor and **published
evidence grade**, never by a measured effect it does not have.

A pleasant side effect: chapter 25's "uncomfortable roadmap consequence" — that
every mechanism for the largest cause of felt pointlessness sat in the
second-to-last stage — is resolved rather than merely noted.

And the PM/UX debate from the fifteenth pass was settled by neither side. Both had
inherited the premise that the menu was a refinement; the manager argued for the
review session, the designer for the status page, and the actual answer was in
neither list. Two well-argued positions can share a wrong premise, and arguing
between them will never surface it. Promoted to `AGENT-PITFALLS.md`.

## Twentieth pass — how difficulty rises, and what "soft block" actually means

### The question

*"How do we measure real progress? When you get really good at vocab, the reading
or correction method should increase accordingly. And you can't show too many
words a person doesn't know."* Then: *"there must be a system behind A1/A2 with
sub-metrics we can just use."*

Half of that is already answered and I had to say so rather than build something:
the level model is a **display, not a controller**, only layer 1 is measured, and
each method calibrates its own difficulty from the learner's holdings. What is
genuinely missing is narrower than it looked — there is no normative table naming
the quantity and band per method, coverage does not say whether it counts lemmas
or forms, and correction and writing have dials with no bands at all.

On the sub-metric system: the authoritative per-language inventories do exist —
the *Plan Curricular* for Spanish, the *Profilo della lingua italiana* for
Italian — and they are licensing-constrained. They can calibrate our bands. They
cannot be republished as requirements, which is the form the user was imagining.

### The distinction that took two attempts

The user's example was *"if someone is not as good at the plural of certain words
you can't give him those in a method"*. That is **material selection inside a
running method**, not a reason to withhold the method — and form mastery is
already a layer-1 signal measured over paradigm cells, with the cell already
carried on every form the lexicon resolves. So the mechanism existed; what was
missing was the sentence saying which of the two problems it solves.

Then: *"maybe say this method is blocked or soft blocked till you get 3/10 in
those vocabs"*. I pushed back, because as a gate it collides with four decisions
at once — A8 (a required station before practice), A9 (a threshold check before
the first exercise is a placement test), E7 (a structure that cannot be declined
is controlling), and the word "unlocked" already meaning content crossing into
coverage reach in UC-033. The user's answer settled it: **"it's not a hard order,
soft block, you can still do it if you click on it."**

### Decisions

- **[D] Readiness may inform and demote. It may never hide and never gate.**
  Three states: **ready** · **better later** (startable, one line of reason) ·
  **no material yet** (nothing exists to start, so the tap opens the explanation
  and the distance).
- **[D] The third state is not a hard block.** The app never refuses; it
  sometimes has nothing to hand over. Reading at coverage with 400 known words
  has no text at 95 % — there is no session there to permit or forbid.
- **[D] "Blocked" and "locked" never reach the screen.** The grammar is F173's —
  the app states the limit of its own knowledge, never a deficit of the learner.
- **[D] Signals have values and statuses. Only skills have levels.** A signal
  rendered as "Plurals: A2" has invented a level the model does not define.
- **[D] Every progress statement runs "this is low because of that", never "do
  that to get this".** The second direction publishes the score to feed and turns
  the display into a controller, which is D1 in a diagnostic costume.
- **[D] A navigation destination is named for the object, not the method** —
  hence words-and-schedule rather than Flashcards, with no count anywhere in the
  navigation (A3, UC-006).

## Twenty-first pass — the critique, and who decides what

Asked for a science-based critique of the readiness system we had just designed.
It came out against parts of my own writing from an hour earlier, which is the
useful outcome.

### What held up

Readiness is a real concept — Pienemann's Teachability Hypothesis says instruction
takes only at the learner's current processing stage. And the architecture the
user was reaching for exists: knowledge components with knowledge tracing, at
d ≈ 0.76 for step-based tutoring in VanLehn's review. It just has nothing to do
with CEFR, whose descriptors were scaled on **teachers' judgments** of difficulty
rather than on learner performance. There is no psychometric substructure under
A2 to read off.

### What did not

Three failures, and the first is the one that matters:

1. **Filtering out items the learner would fail deletes the highest-value
   material.** Errorful retrieval with feedback beats study alone; confidently-held
   errors are the best corrected of all. E3 already draws the correct line — "no
   chance of **retrieval**", not "no chance of success" — and UC-060 as I wrote it
   collapsed the two. Fixed.
2. **Coverage is a comprehension threshold being used as a learning dial.** Hold
   the learner at constant comfort as their vocabulary grows and you have rebuilt
   chapter 25's treadmill from the inside, with good intentions.
3. **A threshold on a per-cell estimate is a decision made on noise**, and
   thresholds are systematically mis-set — Cen et al. found deployed mastery
   thresholds caused over-practice worth around 12 % of practice time, in a system
   with vastly more data per skill than we will ever have.

Plus two narrower ones: a completion fraction pulls effort toward what is left
rather than what pays, which is a Goodhart failure in the geometry of the widget
rather than in anyone's intent (UC-064 fixed); and a recognition metric licenses
no claim about production.

### The decision that resolves it **[D — user, 2026-08-08]**

> **The learner chooses the method. The app chooses what goes inside it.** And
> when the learner asks for content explicitly, that wins too — the steering is
> the default, not a veto.

Which is now thesis 13, because it settles a class of questions rather than one.
The argument that gets there in a sentence: *you have to be able to do the plural
forms* is an argument for putting them in front of the learner, never for making
them wait. Gating produces delay; targeting produces practice.

### Went wrong

Two defects in use cases I had written the same hour, both found by critiquing my
own output rather than by any gate. Worth recording as a pattern: **writing the
use cases first and the evidence chapter second gets the order backwards**, and
the repo's own workflow says evidence precedes contract. The use cases were right
about the shape and wrong in two details that the literature settles immediately.

### Shipped

Chapter 26, thesis 13, a sources section for the readiness and skill-modelling
literature — marked **○ throughout**, because nothing in it was opened during this
pass, with the two figures most in need of checking named — and the two use-case
corrections.

## Twenty-second pass — propagation, and a thousand bots

Two jobs: push chapter 26 through everything it touches, and evaluate the user's
idea of simulating a population of learners.

### Propagation

Thesis 13 into `study/README.md`; a "what a signal may and may not claim" section
into chapter 03, which is where that boundary belongs and where it was missing;
E4 sharpened in chapter 02 (the band is a comprehension threshold, so a method's
purpose picks which end applies); the readiness-versus-context distinction into
chapters 12 and 21, because those two look alike and collapsing them is how a
"cannot perform" rule becomes a "not permitted" rule; **Readiness** and
**Targeting** into the glossary; F193–F201 into the catalogue; questions 20–22
into the roadmap.

### The bots — worth doing, with one trap

The idea is sound and it is a known technique (SimStudent, student simulation for
tutoring systems). The trap is that a simulated learner's learning is **whatever
we programmed**, so any finding of the shape "method X works" is our own input
handed back in chart form. The RL-for-instruction literature is full of policies
that beat baselines in simulation and lose with people.

So [ADR-0008](../adr/0008-simulated-learners-as-a-test-harness.md) bounds it: the
truth is **hidden from the app**, the truth model is a **different functional
family** from FSRS, output may never be cited as evidence about learning, and no
policy may be tuned against it. That last rule is the one that will be tested the
first time someone wants a number they do not have.

What makes it informative rather than circular: the product's central claim is
that it *measures* a level instead of asserting one, and a synthetic learner is
the only way to check an estimate against a truth we know before real users
exist.

### Two bugs the harness found in itself, which is the point

1. **A unit collision.** FSRS stability means "days until recall falls to 0.9";
   my synthetic curve used "days until 1/e". Same word, different scales, so the
   app appeared to catastrophically over-schedule a learner no real person
   resembles — 2,200 suspended tasks per diligent learner. The curve family must
   differ; the unit must not. Fixed by anchoring both at 0.9.
2. **Testing the harness against itself.** The rebuild check compared a due date
   the harness had assigned to new tasks, which is not in the review log, so it
   could never reproduce. Scoped to tasks with at least one review.

Also walked back two assertions that were bounds rather than properties: strict
adjacent-bucket monotonicity failed on a 0.02 inversion that means nothing, and
"the diligent are never suspended" was an absolute where the honest claim is
relative. `SIMULATION.md` rule 2 now says this outright, because the tempting fix
in both cases was to widen the bound.

### The finding worth keeping

Calibration over ~1,000 learners × 365 days: predicted 0.95 → observed ≈ 0.88.
The scheduler is **over-confident at long intervals**, in the direction the
construction predicts, since an exponential truth falls away faster than a power
law past the anchor. Pinned as a test so a change cannot silently reverse it — and
labelled as a property of *our truth model*, not a claim about FSRS or about
people.

The trickler persona also confirms a backlog that a small daily budget cannot
clear. Not a scheduler defect; it is exactly the pressure that produces the
backlog counter A3 forbids, and it now has a number attached before the session
builder is designed.

### Shipped

`lib/simulation/` — seeded RNG, synthetic memory with hidden ground truth, seven
adversarial personas, the runner — plus 13 property tests over 1,000 learners.
`docs/SIMULATION.md`, ADR-0008, and the propagation edits above. `verify` green.

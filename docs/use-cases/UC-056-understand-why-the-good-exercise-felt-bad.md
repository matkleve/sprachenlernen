# UC-056 — Understand why the exercise that worked felt bad

<!-- id: UC-056 -->
<!-- specs:  -->

**Who:** someone who has just finished an effortful, error-rich session and
concluded from the discomfort that the method is not working for them.
**Wants to:** know whether the discomfort meant anything.
**So that:** they do not drop the methods that work in favour of the ones that
feel like they work.

Derived from
[`../study/STUDY-023-why-it-does-not-feel-productive.md`](../study/STUDY-023-why-it-does-not-feel-productive.md)
P2 and P4.

## Today

Learners read mental effort as evidence of poor learning, and then choose the
easier strategy — measured directly: rated less effective, chosen less often, and
in fact retained better. No app says anything at this moment, so the learner's
own explanation is the only one available, and it is systematically the wrong one.

## Success looks like

- After a method that was measurably effortful, **one sentence** appears, once per
  method, ever. Not a tooltip on every session and not a lecture.
- It is about this session and this learner's own data — what the difficulty did —
  rather than a general claim that struggle is good.
- It is **never the only response.** If the learner is in the case where the
  feeling is correct — effort without ever performing a real task — the app owes
  them a task ([UC-055](UC-055-use-the-language-for-something-real.md)), not an
  explanation. Reframing moved perceived effort in the research and did not move
  behaviour.
- Dismissing it is permanent for that method, and there is a way back to it from
  the method's info page.
- The thumbs-down path is unchanged and still asks its follow-up question: "too
  hard" and "boring" have different fixes and this sentence is not a substitute
  for either.

## Out of scope

Any claim that the learner's perception is generally unreliable, motivational
framing, and using the desirable-difficulty argument to defend a session that
contained no retrieval at all.
